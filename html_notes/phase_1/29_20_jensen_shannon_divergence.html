Jensen-Shannon Divergence

Healing the Asymmetry. A smoothed, symmetric version of KL divergence that provides a true distance metric and powers original GAN training.

The Problem with KL

KL Divergence is powerful, but it has two major flaws for certain AI tasks:

  • Asymmetry: It's hard to visualize a "space" where the distance from A to B differs from B to A.
  • Instability: If two distributions don't overlap, KL can become infinite, causing training gradients to explode.

1. The Definition

Jensen-Shannon Divergence (JSD) averages the KL divergence between \(P\) and an "average" distribution \(M\), and \(Q\) and that same average.

$$ JSD(P \| Q) = \frac{1}{2} D_{KL}(P \| M) + \frac{1}{2} D_{KL}(Q \| M) $$ $$ \text{where } M = \frac{1}{2}(P + Q) $$

Symmetry

Unlike KL, JSD is perfectly symmetric. \(JSD(P \| Q) = JSD(Q \| P)\). This makes it feel much more like a physical distance.

Boundedness

JSD is always bounded between 0 and 1 (if using base 2 logs). It never explodes to infinity, even if the distributions have zero overlap.

GANs Nash Equilibrium

The Original GAN Loss

In Ian Goodfellow's original GAN paper, he showed that the min-max game played by the Generator and Discriminator is mathematically equivalent to minimizing the Jensen-Shannon Divergence between the real data distribution and the generated distribution.

$$ \min_G \max_D V(D, G) \equiv \min_G JSD(P_{data} \| P_{generator}) $$

However, even though JSD is bounded, it still suffers from vanishing gradients if the distributions are too far apart (disjoint supports). This insight led to the development of the Wasserstein Distance.

2. KL vs. JSD

Feature KL Divergence JS Divergence
Symmetry No Yes
Bounded No (\([0, \infty)\)) Yes (\([0, 1]\))
Metric No \(\sqrt{JSD}\) is a metric
ML Usage VAEs, Decision Trees GANs, Style Transfer
