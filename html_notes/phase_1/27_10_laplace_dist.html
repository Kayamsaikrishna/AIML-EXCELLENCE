10. Laplace (Double Exponential) Distribution

Sharp peaks and the math of sparsity.

1. Definition

The Laplace Distribution can be thought of as two exponential distributions spliced together back-to-back at the mean $\mu$. It is characterized by its sharp peak and heavy tails (compared to Normal).

$$ f(x) = \frac{1}{2b} e^{-\frac{|x-\mu|}{b}} $$

Where $\mu$ is the location parameter and $b$ is the scale parameter.

2. Statistical Properties

  • Mean: $\mu$
  • Variance: $2b^2$
  • Kurtosis: 6 (higher than Normal's 3, indicating it is "leptokurtic").

3. AI/ML Connection: L1 Regularization and Feature Selection

The "Sparsity" Prior

The Laplace distribution is the mathematical engine behind **L1 Regularization (Lasso)**.

In Machine Learning training, when we add an L1 penalty to our loss function, we are implicitly assuming that the model's weights follow a Laplace distribution centered at 0. Because of the sharp peak at zero, the optimization process is much more likely to push unimportant weights exactly to zero. This performs automatic **Feature Selection**, creating "Sparse" models that are easier to interpret and run faster in production. In **Robust Regression**, the Laplace distribution is used to model residuals that might have extreme outliers, as it is less sensitive to them than the Gaussian-based Least Squares approach.

4. Python: Laplace vs. Normal

import numpy as np
from scipy.stats import laplace, norm

# Compare density at peak
print(f"Laplace peak (b=1): {laplace.pdf(0, loc=0, scale=1):.4f}")
print(f"Normal peak (std=1): {norm.pdf(0, loc=0, scale=1):.4f}")
# Laplace is taller and sharper!

# Sample weights (Sparse simulation)
weights = laplace.rvs(loc=0, scale=0.1, size=10)
print(f"Simulated Sparse Weights:\n{weights.round(3)}")
