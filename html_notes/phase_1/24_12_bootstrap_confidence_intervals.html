Stats 1.4 Percentiles

Bootstrap Confidence Intervals

Converting the histogram of your resampled statistics into a single trustworthy range.

The Histogram Approach

Once you have 1,000 bootstrap estimates (\(\hat{\theta}_1^*, \dots, \hat{\theta}_{1000}^*\)), you don't need Z-tables or T-tables. The data itself tells you which values are common and which are rare.

1. The Percentile Method

For a 95% Confidence Interval: - Lower Bound: The 2.5th percentile of the bootstrap estimates. - Upper Bound: The 97.5th percentile of the bootstrap estimates.

Why it's great: It's intuitive. It handles skewed distributions naturally. It never produces "impossible" values (like a negative variance) as long as your data points are valid.

2. Advanced Methods (BCa)

Sometimes the basic Percentile method is biased if the original data is very small or very skewed.

  • BCa (Bias-Corrected and Accelerated): This is the "Gold Standard" bootstrap interval. It adjusts the percentiles to account for bias in the estimator and the "acceleration" (the rate at which the standard error changes with the parameter).

3. ML Connection: Comparing AUC of Two Models

Deep Learning Research

The Overlap Rule

If you have two models, and Model B's AUC is 0.96 while Model A's is 0.94, how do you know if that 0.02 is real?

Step 1: Calculate the 95% Bootstrap CI for the Difference (Model B AUC - Model A AUC).
Step 2: If the 95% Bootstrap CI for the difference is [0.005, 0.035], you can publish your paper with confidence! Even the "worst-case scenario" (the lower bound) shows Model B is better than Model A.

Cross-Validation Stability

Bootstrap is often used in conjunction with Cross-Validation to estimate the "Variance of the Variance"—measuring how much the CV score itself changes if the dataset was slightly different.
