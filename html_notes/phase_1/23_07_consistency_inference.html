Stats 1.4 Large Sample Truth

Consistency in Inference

Does your estimator eventually hit the bullseye if given infinite data?

The Minimum Requirement

An estimator \(\hat{\theta}_n\) is Consistent if it converges in probability to the true parameter \(\theta\) as the sample size \(n\) goes to infinity.

Consistency is the "bare minimum" for any serious algorithm. If your model doesn't find the truth even with infinite data, it is fundamentally broken.

1. Formal Definition

$$ \hat{\theta}_n \xrightarrow{P} \theta $$ Technically: \(\lim_{n \to \infty} P(|\hat{\theta}_n - \theta| < \epsilon)=1\) for any \(\epsilon> 0\).

2. Consistency vs. Unbiasedness

  • Unbiasedness is about the Average of the estimator for a fixed \(n\).
  • Consistency is about the Convergence of the estimator as \(n \to \infty\).

The Difference

An estimator can be biased but consistent. For example, dividing sample variance by \(n\) is biased, but as \(n \to \infty\), the difference between \(n\) and \(n-1\) vanishes, making the estimator consistent.

3. ML Connection: Asymptotic Correctness

Foundational Convergence

Why "Big Data" Solves Overfitting

Most Machine Learning models (like Logistic Regression or SVMs) are Consistent. This means that in the limit of infinite data, they will recover the "True" decision boundary.

Overfitting is a finite-sample problem. As you add more data, the "Variance" component of the MSE drops to zero (due to consistency), and you are left with only the "Bias" (the error inherent in your choice of model architecture).

4. Summary of Estimator Qualities

Quality Focus Metric
Unbiased Accuracy (on average) \(E[\hat{\theta}] = \theta\)
Efficient Precision (tightness) Smallest Variance
Consistent Reliability (limit) \(\hat{\theta} \to \theta\)
