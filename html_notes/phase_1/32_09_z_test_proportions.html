09. Z-test for Proportions

Benchmarking binary success rates.

1. The Proportion Case

Often we aren't testing a continuous average (like height or score), but a **Binary Rate** (Yes/No, Click/No-Click, Spam/Not-Spam). This follows a Binomial distribution, which can be approximated by a Normal distribution for large samples.

2. The Statistical Test

$$ Z = \frac{\hat{p} - p_0}{\sqrt{\frac{p_0(1-p_0)}{n}}} $$

Where $\hat{p}$ is our observed success rate, $p_0$ is the claimed/null success rate, and $n$ is the sample size. We use the **Null Proportion** ($p_0$) in the denominator for the standard error calculation.

3. AI/ML Connection: CTR Analysis and Ad Recommendation

Evaluating the Click

In AI Marketing, the Z-test for Proportions is the daily workhorse for **Click-Through Rate (CTR) Optimization**.

When you deploy a new **Reinforcement Learning** agent for Ad placement, the $H_0$ is: "The new agent's CTR is no better than 2% ($p_0=0.02$)." If the agent delivers 2,500 clicks out of 100,000 impressions ($\hat{p}=0.025$), a human might say "It's 25% better!". But an AI Scientist runs a **Z-test for Proportions**. Because $n=100,000$ (Very Large), even a 0.5% gain can be **Extremely Significant** ($Z \approx 11$). This allows the system to confidently shift millions of dollars in budget to the new model within minutes of deployment. Conversely, if $n$ was only 500, that 0.5% gain would be statistically invisible, and the Z-test would prevent the AI from making a premature and potentially expensive pivot.

4. Python: proportions_ztest

from statsmodels.stats.proportion import proportions_ztest

# 25 successes out of 500 trials
count = 25
nobs = 500
base_rate = 0.04 # We want to test if it's DIFFERENT from 4%

stat, pval = proportions_ztest(count, nobs, base_rate)

print(f"Z-stat: {stat:.2f}, p-value: {pval:.4f}")
# p = 0.22. We fail to reject H0. No evidence of rate change.
