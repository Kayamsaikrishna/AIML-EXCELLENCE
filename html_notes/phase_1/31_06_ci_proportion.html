06. Confidence Intervals for Proportions

Estimating the probability of success with error bars.

1. The Formula

For a binary outcome (Success/Failure), we estimate the true proportion $p$ using the sample proportion $\hat{p} = \frac{x}{n}$. Using the Normal approximation to the Binomial distribution, the CI is:

$$ \hat{p} \pm Z_{\alpha/2} \sqrt{\frac{\hat{p}(1-\hat{p})}{n}} $$

This is often called the Wald Interval.

2. The "3-Sigma" Rule in AI

In AI, $\hat{p}$ is usually your Model Accuracy. If you test on $n=1,000$ images and get 90% accuracy, the "shakiness" of that 90% depends on the sample size. The term $\sqrt{\frac{\hat{p}(1-\hat{p})}{n}}$ is the Standard Error for proportions. The larger the $n$, the tighter your "Accuracy Error Bars" become.

3. AI/ML Connection: Model Confidence and Accuracy Guarantees

Is 90% really 90%?

In AI, Proportion CIs are the law for Benchmarking.

When you say an AI is "Fair" or "Unbiased," you are usually looking at the proportion of correct predictions across different demographics (e.g., Accuracy for Men vs. Accuracy for Women). If the **95% Confidence Intervals** for these two proportions overlap, you cannot mathematically prove the model is biased—the difference might just be sampling noise. In Production Monitoring, we use Proportion CIs to set "Alert Thresholds." If the model's daily accuracy drops below the lower bound of its historical CI, it's a signal that the data distribution has shifted (Data Drift) and the model needs retraining. This prevents "False Alarms" triggered by small, natural fluctuations in daily data.

4. Python: Accuracy Error Bars

# Model Accuracy: 850 correct out of 1000
p_hat = 850 / 1000
n = 1000

# 95% Confidence
se = np.sqrt((p_hat * (1 - p_hat)) / n)
z_crit = 1.96

low, high = p_hat - z_crit*se, p_hat + z_crit*se

print(f"Reported Accuracy: {p_hat*100:.1f}%")
print(f"95% CI: [{low*100:.1f}%, {high*100:.1f}%]")
# Result: [82.8%, 87.2%]
