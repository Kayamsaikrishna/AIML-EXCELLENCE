Prob 1.4 Exponential Decay

The Chernoff Bound

Achieving exponential sharpness by looking at all moments simultaneously.

The Tightest Ceiling

Markov uses the 1st moment (mean). Chebyshev uses the 2nd moment (variance). Chernoff Bound uses the Moment Generating Function (all moments!).

While Chebyshev gives a decay of \(1/k^2\) (polynomial), Chernoff gives a decay that is exponential (\(e^{-k}\)). This is a "game changer" for proving that systems are reliable.

1. The Formula

For a random variable \(X\) and constant \(a\): $$ P(X \ge a) \le \min_{t > 0} \frac{E[e^{tX}]}{e^{ta}} $$

By choosing the optimal \(t\), we find the sharpest possible bound. For sums of independent variables (like coin flips), this bound is incredibly powerful.

2. Chernoff for Bernoulli Sums

Concentration of Measure

If you flip a fair coin 10,000 times, the probability that the number of heads differs from 5,000 by more than 5% is virtually zero.

Chernoff proves that as you add more independent variables, the distribution "concentrates" extremely tightly around the mean.

3. ML Connection: PAC Learning

Theory Generalization

Probably Approximately Correct (PAC)

In Machine Learning theory, we want to know: "How many samples \(n\) do I need to be 99% sure that my model's error is less than 1%?"

The Chernoff bound is the main tool used to derive these sample complexity bounds. It tells us that the probability of the model being "unlucky" (getting a training error that is far from the true test error) drops exponentially with the number of training samples.

This is why ML works—we don't need infinite data to get high confidence; we just need "enough" for the exponential decay to take over.
