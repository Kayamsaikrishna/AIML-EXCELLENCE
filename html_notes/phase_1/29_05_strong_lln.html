05. Strong Law of Large Numbers (SLLN)

"Almost Sure" convergence: The infinite horizon guarantee.

1. Definition

The Strong Law of Large Numbers is a more powerful version of the WLLN. It states that the sample mean $\bar{X}_n$ converges to the population mean $\mu$ **almost surely**.

$$ P\left( \lim_{n \to \infty} \bar{X}_n = \mu \right) = 1 $$

While the Weak Law says the probability of a mistake goes to zero, the Strong Law says that the "event" of not converging has zero probability. In infinite sequences, the average will hit the truth with 100% certainty.

2. Convergent Paths

In the Weak Law, some "weird" paths could potentially wander away forever, provided they are rare enough. In the Strong Law, every single typical path (trajectory) of the running average must eventually settle down at the mean. This is known as **Trajectory-wise convergence**.

3. AI/ML Connection: Monte Carlo and Stochastic Optimization

The Infinite Learner

The SLLN is the reason why **Monte Carlo Integration** and Stochastic Gradient Descent (SGD) are reliable.

In **Monte Carlo Methods**, we estimate complex integrals (like the expected reward in a game of Go) by averaging millions of random simulations. The SLLN guarantees that as we simulate more, our estimate is mathematically "glued" to the truth. In **Stochastic Optimization**, we update our AI model based on a single random batch of data at a time. The SLLN (combined with ergodicity) ensures that these billions of small, random updates will together converge to the Global Minimum of the loss function, as if we had processed the entire massive dataset in every step. It turns "Random Guessing" into "Infinite Precision."

4. Python: Path Stability

# Simulating path stability across multiple "lifetimes"
n_trials = 1000
n_experiments = 5

for i in range(n_experiments):
    data = np.random.normal(0, 1, n_trials)
    running_avg = np.cumsum(data) / np.arange(1, n_trials + 1)
    plt.plot(running_avg, alpha=0.5)

plt.axhline(0, color='black', lw=2)
plt.title("SLLN: All trajectories converge to 0")
plt.show()
