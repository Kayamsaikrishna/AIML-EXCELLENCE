17. Functions of Random Variables

Analyzing the behavior of mathematical operations on random variables.

1. Linearity of Expectation

One of the most important theorems in probability: the expected value of a sum is the sum of the expected values, regardless of whether the variables are independent.

$$ E[aX + bY] = aE[X] + bE[Y] $$

2. Jensen's Inequality

For a convex function $f$, the expectation of the function is greater than or equal to the function of the expectation:

$$ E[f(X)] \ge f(E[X]) $$

This explains why, for example, the mean of squared values is always greater than the square of the mean (unless variance is zero).

3. AI/ML Connection: Loss Bounds and Variational Inference

The Inequalities of Learning

Functions of random variables define the limits of AI optimization.

**Jensen's Inequality** is the core mathematical engine for **Variational Inference** and **Expectation-Maximization (EM)** algorithms. In Variational Autoencoders (VAEs), we cannot calculate the exact log-likelihood of a complex distribution. Instead, we use Jensen's Inequality to derive the **ELBO (Evidence Lower Bound)**—a function we can optimize that guarantees our model's performance will improve. Furthermore, understanding how the variance of sum functions (like the average gradient over a batch) behaves is what led to the development of **Mini-Batch Gradient Descent**. These mathematical identities are what make scalable Deep Learning possible.

4. Python: Verifying Jensen's Inequality

# Function f(x) = x^2 (Convex)
x = np.random.uniform(1, 10, 1000)

expect_of_func = np.mean(x**2)
func_of_expect = (np.mean(x))**2

print(f"E[X^2]: {expect_of_func:.4f}")
print(f"(E[X])^2: {func_of_expect:.4f}")
print(f"Jensen holds: {expect_of_func >= func_of_expect}")
