Stats 1.4 Interval Estimation

Confidence Intervals (CI): The Concept

Why providing a range is more "honest" than providing a single number.

Point vs. Interval

A Point Estimate (like \(\bar{x} = 5.2\)) is almost certainly wrong. Out of the infinite possible values in the population, the chance it is exactly 5.2 is zero.

An Interval Estimate provides a range of values that we are reasonably sure contains the truth.

1. The Frequentist Interpretation

CRITICAL MISCONCEPTION: A 95% Confidence Interval DOES NOT mean there is a 95% probability that the parameter lies in that specific range.

The actual meaning: If you repeat the experiment 100 times and calculate a CI for each, approximately 95 of those intervals will contain the true (fixed) population parameter.

In Frequentist stats, the Parameter is fixed and the Interval is random (it moves depending on the sample).

2. Anatomoy of a CI

$$ \text{CI} = \text{Point Estimate} \pm (\text{Critical Value} \times \text{Standard Error}) $$
  • Point Estimate: Your best guess (e.g., sample mean).
  • Critical Value: How many "standard deviations" you want to go out (e.g., 1.96 for 95% normal).
  • Standard Error: The uncertainty in your estimate (\(\sigma / \sqrt{n}\)).

3. ML Connection: Error Bars

Visualization Research

The "Honesty" of a Plot

When you see a line plot of "Loss over Time", you often see a shaded area around the line. That shaded area is usually a Confidence Interval.

- If the interval is narrow, the model performance is stable. Any single run of the training process will give roughly similar results. - If the interval is wide, the model is volatile. The "mean accuracy" might be 90%, but in reality, it ranges from 70% to 100% depending on the random seed.

Parameter Significance

In Linear Regression, if the 95% CI for a weight (\(w_i\)) includes zero (e.g., \(w_i \in [-2, 5]\)), it means we cannot be 95% sure that the feature actually helps the prediction. It might just be noise.
