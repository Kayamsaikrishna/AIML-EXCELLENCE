Prob 1.4 The Shapeshifters

RV Transformations (The Change of Variable)

Morphing distributions: scalar scaling to normalizing flows.

The Core Intuition

In Machine Learning, we rarely stare at raw data. We assume the data \(X\) is a transformation of some simpler latent variable \(Z\) (Generative Models), or we transform \(X\) into a more useful shape \(Y\) (Feature Engineering, Batch Norm).

When you transform a Random Variable \(X\) into \(Y = g(X)\), the probability mass/density doesn't just "move"—it stretches or squishes. This distortion must be accounted for to preserve the total probability of 1.

1. Discrete vs. Continuous Transformation

Discrete Case: Just Map It

For discrete RVs, probability mass stays attached to the value.

$$Y = g(X)$$

$$P(Y=y) = \sum_{x: g(x)=y} P(X=x)$$

If multiple \(x\)'s map to the same \(y\), you sum their probabilities. No scaling factor needed.

Continuous Case: The Jacobian

For continuous RVs, we transform density.

$$f_Y(y) = f_X(x) \left| \frac{dx}{dy} \right|$$

The term \(|\frac{dx}{dy}|\) (or \(|\frac{dg^{-1}(y)}{dy}|\)) measures how much the space was stretched. If we stretch space by 2, density drops by half.

Why the Absolute Value?

Probabilities must be positive. Whether \(g(x)\) is increasing or decreasing, an interval \(dx\) maps to \(dy\). We care about the magnitude of the volume change, not the direction, to keep probability mass positive.

2. The Formula (Monotonic Function)

Let \(X\) be a continuous RV with PDF \(f_X(x)\). Let \(Y = g(X)\) be a differentiable, monotonic function. Then the PDF of \(Y\) is: $$f_Y(y) = f_X(g^{-1}(y)) \left| \frac{d}{dy} g^{-1}(y) \right|$$ Where \(x = g^{-1}(y)\).

3. Example: Linear Scaling (Batch Norm Principles)

Let \(X \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2)\). Let \(Y = aX + b\) (Standardizing or Scaling).

Here, \(g(x) = ax+b\), so \(x = \frac{y-b}{a}\). The derivative \(\frac{dx}{dy} = \frac{1}{a}\).

PDF of Y:

$$f_Y(y) = f_X\left(\frac{y-b}{a}\right) \left| \frac{1}{a} \right|$$

This explains why dividing data by \(\sigma\) divides the standard deviation (width) but multiplies the peak height by \(\sigma\), keeping area = 1.

4. ML Connection: Normalizing Flows and VAEs

Deep Learning Generative Models

Normalizing Flows

Normalizing Flows are generative models that construct complex distributions by transforming a simple one (like a Gaussian) through a sequence of invertible functions.

To train them, we need to compute the likelihood of data. This requires calculating the change of variables formula (Determinant of the Jacobian) efficiently. All flow architectures (RealNVP, Glow) are designed specifically so that this determinant is easy to compute.

$$ \log p_X(x) = \log p_Z(z) - \log \left| \det \frac{\partial f}{\partial z} \right| $$
Python: Visualizing Transformation

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
import seaborn as sns

# Original Distribution: Uniform [0, 1]
x = np.random.uniform(0, 1, 10000)

# Transformation: Y = -ln(X) (Inverse Transform Sampling for Exponential)
# If X ~ Uniform(0,1), then -ln(X) ~ Exponential(1)
y = -np.log(x)

plt.figure(figsize=(12, 5))

plt.subplot(1, 2, 1)
sns.histplot(x, bins=50, kde=True, color='skyblue')
plt.title("X ~ Uniform(0, 1)")

plt.subplot(1, 2, 2)
sns.histplot(y, bins=50, kde=True, color='salmon')
plt.title("Y = -ln(X) ~ Exponential(1)")
plt.show()

# Observe: The uniform block is 'stretched' into an asymptotic tail.

5. The Log-Normal Transformation

If \(X \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2)\), then \(Y = e^X\) follows a Log-Normal distribution.

Why it matters: Many natural phenomena (stock prices, weights, incomes) are product-based rather than sum-based. The Central Limit Theorem for multiplication says products of random variables converge to Log-Normal.
