08. Mixed Partial Derivatives & Clairaut's Theorem

Does the order of operations matter?

1. Mixed Partials Definition

A second-order partial derivative can be "mixed", meaning we differentiate with respect to one variable and then another.

$$ f_{xy} = \frac{\partial}{\partial y} \left( \frac{\partial f}{\partial x} \right) , \quad f_{yx} = \frac{\partial}{\partial x} \left( \frac{\partial f}{\partial y} \right) $$

2. Clairaut's Theorem

If the mixed partial derivatives \(f_{xy}\) and \(f_{yx}\) are continuous on a region, then they are equal:

$$ f_{xy} = f_{yx} $$

This is extremely powerful because it means we can pick the "easiest" variable to differentiate first without changing the final result.

3. AI/ML Connection: Hessian Symmetry

Efficient Second-Order Optimization

In advanced AI optimization (like Netwon's Method or Natural Gradient), we use the Hessian Matrix \(\mathbf{H}\), which is the matrix of all second-order partial derivatives.

Thanks to Clairaut's Theorem, the Hessian matrix of a smooth loss function is always Symmetric (\(\mathbf{H} = \mathbf{H}^T\)). This property allows AI scientists to halve the memory needed to store the Hessian and use specialized linear algebra routines (like Eigen-decomposition for symmetric matrices) to find the "Curvature" of the loss landscape. Without this mathematical symmetry, second-order optimization would be twice as slow and twice as memory-intensive.

4. Python: Verifying Symmetry

import sympy as sp

x, y = sp.symbols('x y')
f = x**2 * sp.exp(y) + sp.sin(x*y)

f_xy = sp.diff(sp.diff(f, x), y)
f_yx = sp.diff(sp.diff(f, y), x)

print(f"f_xy: {f_xy}")
print(f"f_yx: {f_yx}")
print(f"Are they equal? {f_xy == f_yx}")
