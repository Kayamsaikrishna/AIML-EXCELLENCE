Prob 1.4 Finite Population

The Hypergeometric Distribution

Sampling without replacement. The Deck of Cards scenario.

Why Binomial isn't enough

The Binomial distribution assumes trials are independent (e.g., flipping a coin, or drawing a card and putting it back).

But what if you draw a card and keep it? The probabilities change! The probability of drawing an Ace changes from 4/52 to 3/51.
When the population is small and we sample without replacement, we use the Hypergeometric Distribution.

1. The Setup

  • Population size: \(N\)
  • Total Successes in population: \(K\)
  • Sample size (draws): \(n\)
  • Number of observed successes: \(k\) (The Random Variable)
The PMF is formed by combinatorics (choose logic): $$ P(X=k) = \frac{\binom{K}{k} \binom{N-K}{n-k}}{\binom{N}{n}} $$
Logic:
  • \(\binom{K}{k}\): Choose \(k\) successes from the \(K\) available.
  • \(\binom{N-K}{n-k}\): Choose the remaining \(n-k\) failures from the \(N-K\) available.
  • \(\binom{N}{n}\): Total ways to choose any \(n\) items.

2. ML Connection: Goodness of Fit & Testing

Statistics Fisher's Exact Test

Fisher's Exact Test

In A/B testing or biological studies, we often have a 2x2 contingency table (e.g., Treated vs Placebo, Cured vs Not Cured) with small sample sizes.

To calculate if the treatment had a significant effect, we can't assume a normal approximation. We assume fixed marginal totals and calculate the exact probability of observing such an extreme distribution of outcomes. This probability follows the Hypergeometric distribution.

3. Large Population Limit

If the population \(N\) is huge relative to the sample \(n\) (e.g., \(N=1,000,000\), \(n=100\)), removing one item barely changes the probabilities.

As \(N \to \infty\), Hypergeometric \(\to\) Binomial.
We act as if we are sampling with replacement because the "without replacement" effect is negligible.
