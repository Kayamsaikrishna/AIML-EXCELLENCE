Stats 1.8 Rigorous Design

Power Analysis

Eliminating "Maybe". How to ensure your experiment is strong enough to detect the truth.

The Four Levers

Power analysis is like a 4-dial machine. If you know any three, you can calculate the fourth:
1. Sample Size (\(n\)): How many people are in the test? 2. Effect Size (\(d\)): How big is the change you expect to see? 3. Significance Level (\(\alpha\)): Your tolerance for False Positives (Usually 0.05). 4. Power (\(1-\beta\)): Your probability of detecting an effect that actually exists (Usually 0.80).

1. Error Types Refresher

Type I Error (\(\alpha\))

False Positive: You say "It works!" but it was just noise.
*Penalty:* Wasted engineering effort launching a useless feature.

Type II Error (\(\beta\))

False Negative: You say "Nothing happened," but there was a real win you missed.
*Penalty:* Missing out on growth or revenue.

2. The Formula Logic

$$ n \approx \frac{16 \cdot \sigma^2}{\delta^2} \quad (\text{For } \alpha=0.05, \text{ Power}=0.8) $$

Where \(\delta\) is the Minimum Detectable Effect (MDE).

Notice the squared relationship: To detect an effect that is half as big, you need **four times as much data**.

3. ML Connection: Sample Size Planning

Product ML Validation

Why "Big Data" still needs Power

Engineers often think: "We have millions of users, we don't need power analysis!"

  • The Micro-Effect Problem: In mature products (Google Search, Amazon Ads), a "huge" win might only be a 0.1% increase in revenue. To detect a 0.1% increase with confidence, you might still need tens of millions of users. Power analysis tells you how many weeks to wait.
  • Data Collection Costs: In medical AI or robotics, data is expensive (Human labeling, physical sensor time). Power analysis prevents you from over-collecting data, saving thousands of dollars.
  • Iterative Refinement: If your A/B test results are "Insignificant," power analysis helps you decide: "Was the change useless, or was my sample size just too small?"
Engineer's Rule: Always perform an A-priori Power Analysis *before* you start the experiment. Doing it after the data is collected (Post-hoc) is considered statistically invalid.
