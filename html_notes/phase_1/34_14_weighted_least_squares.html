14. Weighted Least Squares (WLS)

Giving more importance to more reliable data.

1. The Problem of Heteroscedasticity

Standard OLS assumes every data point has the same variance (noise level). But often, "Bigger" values have "Bigger" errors (e.g., predicting the spending of a millionaire vs. a student). In these cases, OLS is no longer the Best Linear Unbiased Estimator (BLUE).

2. The Solution: Weighting

Weighted Least Squares (WLS) assigns a weight $w_i$ to each data point. We minimize the weighted sum of squares:

$$ \text{Minimize } \sum_{i=1}^{n} w_i (y_i - \hat{y}_i)^2 $$

Setting $w_i = 1/\sigma_i^2$ (inverse of the variance) gives more weight to precise observations, restoring the "BLUE" status to the model.

3. AI/ML Connection: Imbalanced Learning and Error Weighting

Paying Attention to what Matters

In AI, WLS is the concept behind Cost-Sensitive Learning.

When training an AI on **Imbalanced Datasets** (e.g., Fraud detection where 99% of cases are 'Not Fraud'), we use weights to "Force" the model to pay 1,000x more attention to the rare fraud cases. This is technically a form of Weighted Least Squares. In Active Learning**, the AI identifies which points it is most "Confident" about and gives them higher weights during the update cycle. Furthermore, **Uncertainty-Aware Neural Networks** use WLS-style gates to ignore "Noisy" inputs during inference, ensuring that the final output is based on the highest-quality signals in the vector. It is the mathematical way of telling the AI: "Trust, but verify."

4. Python: Fitting with Weights

# weights = [10, 1, 1, 10] 
# First and last points are 10x more 'important'
model = LinearRegression().fit(X, y, sample_weight=weights)

# The resulting line will be 'pulled' more strongly 
# toward the high-weight points.
