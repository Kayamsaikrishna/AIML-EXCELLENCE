Stats 1.5 F-Test

ANOVA (One-Way)

Comparing three or more groups simultaneously to avoid the trap of multiple testing.

Why not just do many T-tests?

If you have 5 models and you compare every pair (10 tests), your chance of a False Positive (Type I Error) skyrockets from 5% to ~40%!

ANOVA (Analysis of Variance) tests all groups at once with a single P-value.

1. The Logic

$$ F = \frac{\text{Variance BETWEEN Groups}}{\text{Variance WITHIN Groups}} $$

- If the groups are far apart (High Between) and tight inside (Low Within), the \(F\)-score is high. - High \(F\) = Reject the Null (There is a difference somewhere).

2. The ANOVA Table

Source Sum of Squares df Mean Square F
Between SSB k - 1 MSB = SSB/df MSB / MSW
Within SSW N - k MSW = SSW/df -

(\(k\) = number of groups, \(N\) = total sample size).

3. ML Connection: Comparing Architectures

Deep Learning Validation

Which Model Structure is Best?

You are testing 4 different neural architectures: 1. CNN 2. ResNet 3. Vision Transformer (ViT) 4. MLP-Mixer

You run 10 training sessions for each (accounting for weight init randomness). - Null Hypothesis (\(H_0\)): All 4 architectures have the same mean accuracy. - Alternative (\(H_a\)): At least ONE architecture is different.

Running an ANOVA allows you to say "Yes, there is a statistically significant difference between these four methods" without cheating the P-value by doing 6 individual pair-wise comparisons.

Note: ANOVA only tells you that *a difference exists*. It doesn't tell you *which* group is different. For that, you need a follow-up Tukey Post-Hoc Test.
