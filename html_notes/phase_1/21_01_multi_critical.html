01. Extreme Values and Critical Points

Finding the mountain peaks and valley floors.

1. Definitions

A function \(f(x, y)\) has a Local Maximum at \((a, b)\) if \(f(x, y) \leq f(a, b)\) for all points in a small disk around \((a, b)\). Likewise for a Local Minimum. These are collectively known as Local Extrema.

A point \((a, b)\) is a Critical Point of \(f\) if:

  • The gradient is zero: \(\nabla f(a, b) = \mathbf{0}\) (meaning \(f_x = 0\) AND \(f_y = 0\)).
  • OR, one of the partial derivatives does not exist.

2. Fermat's Theorem for Multi-Variables

If \(f\) has a local maximum or minimum at \((a, b)\) and the first-order partial derivatives exist there, then \((a, b)\) must be a critical point.

Note: Just like in 1D, not every critical point is an extremum! It could be a Saddle Point.

3. AI/ML Connection: Loss Landscapes

The Search for the Global Minimum

In Supervised Learning, we define a "Loss Function" \(L(\mathbf{w})\). Training the model is simply the process of finding the Global Minimum of this function.

Because these functions exist in millions of dimensions, we can't solve \(\nabla L = 0\) algebraically. Instead, we use Gradient Descent to "walk" towards a critical point. A major challenge in AI is that the landscape is full of "Local Minima" (valleys that aren't the deepest) and "Saddle Points" (flat regions) that can trap the model's progress. Understanding the math of critical points helps researchers design better ways to "Jump" out of bad valleys.

4. Python: Symbolic Critical Points

import sympy as sp

x, y = sp.symbols('x y')
f = x**2 + y**2 - 2*x - 6*y + 14

# Solve grad(f) = 0
fx = sp.diff(f, x)
fy = sp.diff(f, y)

critical_points = sp.solve([fx, fy], (x, y))
print(f"Critical Point: {critical_points}")
# Result: {x: 1, y: 3}
