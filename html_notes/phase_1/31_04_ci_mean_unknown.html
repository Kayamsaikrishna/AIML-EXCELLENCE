04. CI for Population Mean (Unknown Variance)

The t-Interval: Handling "Double Uncertainty."

1. The Formula

In almost all real-world AI projects, we do not know the population variance $\sigma^2$ beforehand. We must estimate it from the sample using $s$. This adds a second layer of uncertainty, requiring us to use the t-distribution instead of the Z-distribution.

$$ \bar{x} \pm t_{\alpha/2, n-1} \left( \frac{s}{\sqrt{n}} \right) $$
  • $\bar{x}$: Sample Mean.
  • $t_{\alpha/2, n-1}$: Critical value from the t-distribution with $n-1$ degrees of freedom.
  • $s / \sqrt{n}$: Sample Standard Error.

2. Why t is wider than Z

The t-distribution has "fatter tails" than the Normal distribution. This wider range reflects our lack of knowledge about the true variance. As the sample size $n$ increases, our estimate of $s$ becomes so good that the t-distribution slowly merges with the Standard Normal Z-distribution. For $n > 100$, the difference is negligible.

3. AI/ML Connection: Experimental Reliability and Model Choice

The Truth about Error Bars

In AI, the Unknown-Variance CI is the **Professional Standard** for reporting results.

When you read a research paper that says "The model achieved $92\% \pm 1.2\%$ accuracy," they are almost always using a t-interval. This is critical for Comparing Models. If Model A's CI and Model B's CI overlap significantly, you cannot claim Model A is better—you simply have too little data to distinguish them. In Early Stage Training, where you might only have 10-20 "Golden Samples" for a niche task, using the t-interval instead of the Z-interval prevents you from being over-optimistic about your model's stability. It forces you to respect the fact that with small data, your estimate of the "noise" is itself noisy.

4. Python: Calculating the t-Interval

from scipy import stats

# Data: Latency of an API call in milliseconds (n=12)
latencies = [45, 52, 48, 41, 60, 55, 49, 44, 51, 53, 47, 50]
n = len(latencies)
mu_hat = np.mean(latencies)
s_hat = np.std(latencies, ddof=1)

# 95% Confidence (df = 11)
t_crit = stats.t.ppf(0.975, df=n-1)

margin_of_error = t_crit * (s_hat / np.sqrt(n))
ci = (mu_hat - margin_of_error, mu_hat + margin_of_error)

print(f"Sample Mean: {mu_hat:.2f}")
print(f"95% CI (t-dist): ({ci[0]:.2f}, {ci[1]:.2f})")
