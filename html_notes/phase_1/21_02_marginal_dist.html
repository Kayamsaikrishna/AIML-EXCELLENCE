Prob 1.4 The Shadow

Marginal Distributions

Compressing the joint distribution to isolate a single variable's behavior.

Projection and Shadows

Imagine a 3D object casting a 2D shadow on a wall. The 3D object is the Joint Distribution (all features combined). The 2D shadow is the Marginal Distribution (looking at one feature while ignoring the rest).

"Marginal" comes from the practice of writing the row/column totals in the margins of a probability table.

1. Discrete Case: Summing Out

Given a joint PMF \(p(x, y)\), the marginal PMF of \(X\) is: $$ p_X(x) = \sum_y p(x, y) $$

Intuition: To find the probability that \(X = 2\), you look at the row where \(X=2\) and add up the probabilities for all possible \(Y\) values in that row. You are effectively "averaging out" the influence of \(Y\).

2. Continuous Case: Integrating Out

Given a joint PDF \(f(x, y)\), the marginal PDF of \(X\) is: $$ f_X(x) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} f(x, y) \, dy $$

Visual: If you have a 3D "mountain" of joint density, the marginal PDF is what you see when you look at the mountain from the side (the profile or silhouette). Integration collapses the volume along one axis onto the other.

3. ML Connection: Latent Variables & GMMs

Clustering Hidden Markov Models

Marginalizing Latent Variables

In models like Gaussian Mixture Models (GMMs), we assume our data \(X\) is generated by some unobserved "latent" variable \(Z\) (which cluster the point belongs to).

The likelihood of our data \(P(X)\) is actually a Marginal Probability: $$ P(X) = \sum_z P(X, Z) = \sum_z P(X \mid Z) P(Z) $$ We often marginalize out these hidden variables to find the overall distribution of the observed data.

Dimensionality Reduction

Technically, ignoring a column in your pandas dataframe is a form of marginalization. You are deciding to look at the distribution of 'Price' without conditioning it on 'Location' or 'Size'.

4. The Marginalization Trick

A beautiful property of the Multivariate Normal Distribution is that its marginals are always Normal. If you have a 100-dimensional Gaussian and you only look at one dimension, that dimension is a simple 1D Gaussian. This is NOT true for all distributions!
