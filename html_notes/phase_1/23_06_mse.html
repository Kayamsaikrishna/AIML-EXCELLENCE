Stats 1.4 Total Quality

Mean Squared Error (MSE)

Measuring the "closeness" of an estimator to the truth, accounting for both accuracy and precision.

The Total Error

Being unbiased is great, but it's not everything. An estimator could be unbiased but have a massive variance, meaning any single guess could be wildly wrong. MSE captures the total "badness" of an estimator.

1. The Formula

$$ \text{MSE}(\hat{\theta}) = E[(\hat{\theta} - \theta)^2] $$

2. The Bias-Variance Decomposition

This is one of the most important equations in all of Data Science: $$ \text{MSE}(\hat{\theta}) = \text{Bias}(\hat{\theta})^2 + \text{Var}(\hat{\theta}) $$

The Trade-off

To reduce total error (MSE), you have two knobs: 1. Reduce Bias: Make your model more complex to capture reality better. 2. Reduce Variance: Make your model simpler so it doesn't over-react to noise in the specific sample.

Often, you can only reduce one by increasing the other. This is the Bias-Variance Trade-off.

3. ML Connection: Regularization

Overfitting Ridge/Lasso

The "Shrinkage" Strategy

In Ridge Regression (L2), we intentionally add a small amount of Bias by pushing weights towards zero. Why? Because this drastically reduces the Variance of the weights. If the reduction in variance is greater than the increase in bias-squared, the Total Error (MSE) goes down.

This is why Regularized models often generalize better to new data than unconstrained ones.
