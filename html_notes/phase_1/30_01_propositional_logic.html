Formal Logic Foundations

Propositional Logic

Grammar of Truth. The foundation of all mathematical reasoning, providing the syntax and rules for building complex logical statements from simple observations.

The Atomic Statement

Propositional Logic deals with propositions: statements that are either True (T) or False (F), but never both or neither. It ignores "what" the statement says and focuses entirely on the "logic skeleton" that connects them.

1. Defining Propositions

What IS a Proposition?

  • "The sky is blue" (T)
  • "2 + 2 = 5" (F)
  • "Python is a language" (T)

What IS NOT a Proposition?

  • "Go to the store!" (Command)
  • "X + 1 = 2" (Predicate - depends on X)
  • "This sentence is false." (Paradox)

2. Symbolic Representation

We represent propositions using letters like \(p, q, r, s\). This allows us to perform "algebra on truth" without getting bogged down in linguistics.

Example:

\(p\): "The sun is shining."

\(q\): "I am happy."

Logic statement: If \(p\), then \(q\) (\(p \rightarrow q\)).

LLMs Prompting

Why Logic matters in AI

Current AI models (LLMs) are often described as "stochastic parrots," but their ability to reason depends on following internal logical consistency.

  • Chain-of-Thought (CoT): By forcing an AI to break down a problem into a series of propositions, we increase the probability that it arrives at a logically sound conclusion.
  • Consistency Checks: Many AI evaluation frameworks use propositional logic to verify if an AI's claims on Page 1 are consistent with its claims on Page 10.
  • Knowledge Graphs: Large-scale symbolic AI relies on storing facts as interconnected propositions (\(subject \xrightarrow{relation} object\)).
