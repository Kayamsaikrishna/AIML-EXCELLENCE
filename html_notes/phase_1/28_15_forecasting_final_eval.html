Stats 1.8 Accuracy

Forecasting Evaluation

Judging the Oracle. How to mathematically prove that your model isn't just lucky.

The Truth in the Future

In standard ML, we have a Test Set. In Time Series, we have a Hold-out Window.

We train on data until Time \(T\), and then we see how well our forecast matches the actual events at Time \(T+1, T+2...\).

1. Error Metrics: The Three Kings

1. MAE & RMSE

MAE: Mean Absolute Error.
RMSE: Root Mean Squared Error.
These are "Scale-Dependent". If you are predicting house prices in Millions, the error will be in the thousands. Use them to compare different models on the same dataset.

2. MAPE

Mean Absolute Percentage Error.
Useful because it's a percentage (e.g., "We are 95% accurate").
Danger: It breaks if the actual value is zero, and it heavily penalizes forecasts that are "too high" more than "too low".

3. MASE

Mean Absolute Scaled Error.
The "Scientist's Choice". It compares your model's error to a "Naive" forecast (just guessing that tomorrow will be the same as today). If MASE < 1, your model is better than a coin flip.

2. Diagnostic Check: Ljung-Box

$$ Q = n(n+2) \sum_{k=1}^h \frac{\hat{\rho}_k^2}{n-k} $$

After building a model, look at the Residuals (the errors). If the residuals have patterns, you missed something!

  • Ljung-Box Test: Checks if there is any Autocorrelation left in the residuals.
  • Ideally: Residuals should be White Noise (Normal distribution centered at zero with no correlation).

3. ML Connection: Business Loss Functions

Product ML Supply Chain

Why RMSE often fails the business

Machine Learning models usually minimize RMSE because it's easy for calculus. But businesses have Asymmetric Risk.

  • Inventory Management: If you under-forecast demand, you lose a sale (Cost of Stockout). If you over-forecast, you have wasted storage (Cost of Inventory). These costs are rarely equal.
  • Quantile Regression: Instead of predicting the *mean*, we often use Pinball Loss to predict the 90th percentile of demand. This ensures we almost never run out of stock, even if it means we over-predict slightly.
  • Backtesting: Always use a "Rolling" or "Expanding" window backtest. A model that worked locally in April might fail in November. Multiple "Time-Slices" are the only way to prove robustness.
Final Mastery: A lower RMSE does not always mean a better model. Always visualize the forecast against the actuals. A model that "misses" slightly but follows the general shape of the trend is often more trustworthy than one with low error that predicts a flat line.
