Prob 1.4 The Superpower

Expectations of Multiple Random Variables

Calculus of averages for high-dimensional data.

The Mathematical Magic of Addition

The most important theorem in all of probability is Linearity of Expectation.

It states that the expected value of a sum is the sum of the expected values, REGARDLESS of whether the variables are independent or not.

1. Linearity of Expectation

For any random variables \(X_1, X_2, \dots, X_n\) and constants \(a_i\): $$ E[a_1 X_1 + a_2 X_2 + \dots + a_n X_n] = a_1 E[X_1] + a_2 E[X_2] + \dots + a_n E[X_n] $$

Why is this a big deal? Summing variables is easy, but calculating their joint distribution is hard. Because expectation is linear, we can ignore the complex dependencies (correlations) between variables if we only care about their sum's average.

2. Expectation of a Product

If \(X\) and \(Y\) are Independent, then: $$ E[XY] = E[X] E[Y] $$

Warning: This is generally NOT true if they are dependent. The difference \(E[XY] - E[X]E[Y]\) is actually how we define Covariance (coming in the next module!).

3. ML Connection: Expected Loss

Deep Learning Optimization

Stochastic Gradient Descent (SGD)

In deep learning, we want to minimize the Global Loss: $$ L(\theta) = E_{x,y \sim \mathcal{D}} [ \ell(f(x; \theta), y) ] $$ Calculating this across millions of data points is impossible.

Linearity of expectation allows us to use Mini-batches. The expectation of the gradient calculated on a small batch is an unbiased estimator of the true global gradient.

4. Variance of a Sum

Unlike Expectation, Variance is NOT linear (unless variables are independent).

$$ \text{Var}(X + Y) = \text{Var}(X) + \text{Var}(Y) + 2\text{Cov}(X, Y) $$

The Diversification Principle: In finance, you want to invest in assets with negative covariance. If Asset A goes down while Asset B goes up, the sum of their returns stays stable, reducing total variance (risk).
