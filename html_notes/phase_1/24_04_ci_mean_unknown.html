Stats 1.4 T-Interval

CI for Mean (Unknown \(\sigma\))

How we adjust for the double-uncertainty of not knowing the mean AND not knowing the variance.

The Realistic Case

In nearly all real-world Machine Learning problems, we don't know the "True Variance" (\(\sigma^2\)) of the error. We only have the Sample Variance (\(s^2\)) calculated from our limited dataset.

Because \(s\) is just an estimate, we must use a slightly wider interval to account for this extra layer of guesswork.

1. The Formula

$$ \bar{x} \pm t_{\alpha/2, n-1} \left( \frac{s}{\sqrt{n}} \right) $$
  • \(s\): Sample Standard Deviation.
  • \(t_{\alpha/2, n-1}\): Critical value from the Student's T-distribution.
  • \(n-1\): Degrees of Freedom (df).

2. Degrees of Freedom (df)

The more data you have, the more you can "trust" your estimate of the variance. - For small \(n\), the T-distribution has fat tails (it expects more outliers because our variance estimate might be too low). - As \(n \to \infty\), the T-distribution converges to the Normal (Z) distribution.

3. ML Connection: Benchmarking Small Models

Deep Learning Few-Shot Learning

The Danger of Small Test Sets

If you are testing an LLM on a specialized dataset with only 30 examples, you cannot use Z-stats. The uncertainty in your measurement of "Average Pass Rate" is high.

Using the T-Interval ensures that your error bars reflect the factual reality: that with only 30 samples, your estimate of the model's reliability is itself highly variable.
