02. Normal (Gaussian) Distribution

The heart of statistics and the bell curve of reality.

1. Definition

A random variable $X$ follows a Normal Distribution with mean $\mu$ and variance $\sigma^2$, denoted $X \sim N(\mu, \sigma^2)$, if its PDF is:

$$ f(x) = \frac{1}{\sigma \sqrt{2\pi}} e^{-\frac{1}{2} \left( \frac{x-\mu}{\sigma} \right)^2} $$

It is symmetric around the mean $\mu$, which is also the mode and median.

2. The Empirical Rule (68-95-99.7)

For any normal distribution:

  • ~68.2% of data is within $1\sigma$ of the mean.
  • ~95.4% of data is within $2\sigma$ of the mean.
  • ~99.7% of data is within $3\sigma$ of the mean.

3. AI/ML Connection: The Default Assumption

Why Gaussian Everywhere?

The Normal distribution is the default "Prior" for almost all AI systems.

In **Linear Regression**, the mathematical derivation of the Ordinary Least Squares (OLS) solution assumes that the "Errors" (Residuals) follow a Normal distribution with $\mu=0$. In **Natural Language Processing**, the Gaussian Error Linear Unit (GELU) activation function is used in models like BERT and GPT. Furthermore, the Central Limit Theorem guarantees that if you sum many independent random effects (like the millions of operations in a neural network layer), the result tends to be Gaussian. This is why we initialize weights using distributions like Xavier/He Normal—it ensures the signals start in a balanced, mathematically predictable state.

4. Python: Working with Gaussian PDF

import numpy as np
from scipy.stats import norm

# Normal distribution with mu=100, sigma=15 (IQ score example)
mu, sigma = 100, 15

# Probability of having exactly an IQ of 100 (density)
print(f"Density at mean: {norm.pdf(100, mu, sigma):.4f}")

# Probability of IQ < 130 (2 standard deviations above mean)
print(f"P(X < 130): {norm.cdf(130, mu, sigma):.4f}") # ~0.9772
