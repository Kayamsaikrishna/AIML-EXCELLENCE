Stats 1.5 Small Samples

T-Test (Single Mean)

The standard tool for everyday science when you don't know the population's true volatility.

The Real-World Scenario

In the real world, we rarely know the population standard deviation (\(\sigma\)). We have to estimate it from our sample (\(s\)).

This estimation introduces extra uncertainty, which we handle by using the **Student's T-Distribution** instead of the Normal Distribution.

1. The Test Statistic

$$ t = \frac{\bar{x} - \mu_0}{s / \sqrt{n}} $$ $$ df = n - 1 $$
  • \(s\): Sample Standard Deviation.
  • \(df\): Degrees of Freedom.

2. T-test vs. Z-test

Feature Z-test T-test
Population Sigma (\(\sigma\)) Known Unknown
Sample Size Large (\(n \ge 30\)) Any size (Small preferred)
Distribution Used Standard Normal (\(Z\)) Student's T

3. ML Connection: Expensive Training Runs

Deep Learning Resource Optimization

Small Sample Benchmarks

If training a Large Language Model (LLM) costs $50,000, you cannot afford to train it 1,000 times to get a "Large Sample." You might only train it 5 times with different seeds.

To test if a new optimizer significantly reduces final loss: 1. Calculate loss across 5 seeds. 2. Use a T-test since \(n=5\) is small and \(\sigma\) is unknown.

The T-test is more conservative (requires a much higher difference to prove significance) because it acknowledges that with only 5 data points, your estimate of "volatility" (\(s\)) might be way off.
