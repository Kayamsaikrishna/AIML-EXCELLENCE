08. Chi-Square ($\chi^2$) Distribution

The math of squared deviations and independence.

1. Definition

If $Z_1, Z_2, \dots, Z_k$ are $k$ independent **Standard Normal** random variables, then the sum of their squares follows a Chi-Square Distribution with $k$ degrees of freedom.

$$ X = \sum_{i=1}^k Z_i^2 \sim \chi^2(k) $$

The distribution is always positive and is skewed to the right (though it becomes more symmetric as $k$ increases).

2. Statistical Properties

  • Mean: $E[X] = k$
  • Variance: $Var(X) = 2k$
  • Additive Property: If $X \sim \chi^2(k_1)$ and $Y \sim \chi^2(k_2)$, then $X+Y \sim \chi^2(k_1+k_2)$.

3. AI/ML Connection: Feature Selection and Data Validation

Reducing Dimensionality

The Chi-Square distribution is the "Gatekeeper" of **Feature Importance** in AI.

In **Feature Selection**, we use the **Chi-Square Test** to determine if a categorical feature (like "City" or "User Gender") is independent of the target label. If the result is a high Chi-Square value (above the critical threshold), the feature is likely important; if low, it's just noise and can be deleted to simplify the model. It is also used in **Goodness-of-Fit** tests to check if the AI's predicted probabilities match the distribution of the actual data. For example, if your AI predicts "Probability of Error" across 5 categories, the Chi-Square distribution helps you mathematically prove if the AI's "Mental Map" matches reality.

4. Python: Chi-Square Test for Independence

from scipy.stats import chi2_contingency

# Contingency table (e.g., Click vs No-Click for 2 ad designs)
#        Design A | Design B
# Click:    20    |    40
# Skip :   100    |    100

table = [[20, 40], [100, 100]]
chi2, p, dof, expected = chi2_contingency(table)

print(f"Chi2 statistic: {chi2:.4f}")
print(f"P-value: {p:.4f}") # If p < 0.05, the design MATTERS.
