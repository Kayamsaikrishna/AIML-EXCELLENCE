Relative Entropy & Bayesian Inference

Measuring the Surprise of Discovery. How Relative Entropy (KL Divergence) quantifies the information gained during the cycle of Bayesian updating.

The Information Gain of Data

In Bayesian statistics, we start with a Prior (our guess) and see Data. The data transforms our guess into a Posterior. The Relative Entropy between the Posterior and the Prior is exactly the amount of information we extracted from the data.

1. Bayesian Updating as Information Transfer

The Informative Prior

If our Prior is already very accurate, the new data won't change it much. The KL divergence is low. We say the "Relative Information" of the data is small.

The Surprise of Data

If the data contradicts our Prior, our beliefs shift drastically. The KL divergence is high. This is where learning happens.

2. Sanov's Theorem: Large Deviations

Relative Entropy also answers a deep statistical question: If I flip a fair coin 100 times, how likely am I to see 70 heads?

$$ P(\text{Empirical Dist } \approx P) \sim 2^{-n D_{KL}(P \| Q)} $$

The probability of seeing a "weird" distribution falls exponentially with the Relative Entropy between the observed frequency and the true probability. This is why KL is the natural language of large-scale statistical testing.

Active Learning Bayesian NNs

Application in Modern AI

  • Active Learning (BALD): We want to label the most "informative" data points. One common strategy is to pick points that are expected to maximize the KL Divergence between our current model weights and the updated weights. We look for samples that cause the most "Bayesian Surprise."
  • PAC-Bayes Bounds: Theoretical researchers use KL divergence between a learned model and a prior to put an upper bound on how much the model might overfit. A small KL means the model hasn't "memorized" the data too much.
  • Variational Objectives: As we've seen, VI is just a way to perform Bayesian inference by minimizing relative entropy.
