05. Alternating Series & Leibniz Test

Dealing with the "tug-of-war" of changing signs.

1. What is an Alternating Series?

An alternating series has terms that alternate in sign: positive, then negative, then positive, etc. It looks like \(\sum (-1)^n a_n\).

2. The Leibniz Test

An alternating series converges if two simple conditions are met:

  1. Decreasing: The magnitude of the terms gets smaller (\(a_{n+1} \leq a_n\)).
  2. Vanishing Limit: The terms approach zero (\(\lim a_n = 0\)).

This is much "easier" than regular series. An alternating series can converge even if its non-alternating version (Absolute value) diverges. This is called Conditional Convergence.

3. AI/ML Connection: SGD Oscillations & Momentum

The Path of Learning

When training an AI with Stochastic Gradient Descent (SGD), the weights often "overshoot" the target. In one step the weight increases, in the next it decreases to correct itself. This creates an Alternating Sequence of updates.

To ensure the AI eventually settles on a correct answer rather than oscillating forever, we reduce the Learning Rate over time. This mimics the Leibniz Test conditions (\(a_n\) decreasing and going to zero). By controlling the alternating nature of these updates, AI engineers prevent the model from "Diverging" and ensure that even with noisy data, the model eventually stabilizes. This is the logic behind Annealing and Learning Rate Schedulers.

4. Python: Alternating Sum Convergence

# Alternating Harmonic Series: 1 - 1/2 + 1/3 - 1/4...
# Converges to ln(2) approx 0.693
import numpy as np

total = 0
for n in range(1, 1001):
    total += ((-1)**(n+1)) / n

print(f"Sum (1000 terms): {total:.6f}")
print(f"Verify (ln 2): {np.log(2):.6f}")
