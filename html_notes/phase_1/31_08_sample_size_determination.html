08. Sample Size Determination

How much data is "Enough"?

1. The Core Equation

To plan a study, we often start with the **Marginal Error ($E$)** we are willing to tolerate. To find the required sample size $n$ to achieve that error with a specific confidence level, we rearrange the CI formula:

$$ E = Z_{\alpha/2} \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}} \implies n = \left( \frac{Z_{\alpha/2} \sigma}{E} \right)^2 $$

Note that $n$ increases with the **Square** of the precision. To cut your error in half, you need four times as much data.

2. Proportions and the "Worst Case"

For proportions, $n = \frac{Z^2 \hat{p}(1-\hat{p})}{E^2}$. If you have no idea what $\hat{p}$ is, we use the "conservative" estimate of 0.5, which maximizes the product $\hat{p}(1-\hat{p})$ at 0.25. This ensures your study is powered even if the result is a coin flip.

3. AI/ML Connection: Dataset Budgeting and Labeling

The Economics of Information

In AI, Sample Size Selection is the math of **Profitability**.

Labeling data is expensive. If you are building a specialized LLM for legal documents, a high-quality human labeler might cost $50 per hour. If your project manager asks: "How many documents do we need to label to ensure our model's accuracy is within 1% of the truth?", you use this formula. It prevents **Over-Labeling** (spending millions on data you don't need) and **Under-Labeling** (spending thousands only to find your final model's accuracy is too shaky to deploy). In **Active Learning**, we use these calculations to decide when it's time to stop training—if the "Margin of Error" on our validation set is already smaller than our business tolerance, further labeling provides diminishing returns.

4. Python: Calculating Required n

# Goal: Accuracy within +/- 0.02 (2%) with 95% confidence
e = 0.02
z = 1.96

# Assuming p=0.5 (Worst Case)
n_required = (z**2 * 0.5 * 0.5) / (e**2)

print(f"Required samples for +/- 2%: {int(np.ceil(n_required))}")
# Result: 2401 samples
