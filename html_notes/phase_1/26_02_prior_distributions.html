Stats 1.6 Pre-Data Knowledge

Prior Distributions

Quantifying what you know before you see a single data point.

The Subjectivity Argument

Critics of Bayesianism hate Priors because they seem "subjective." But Bayesians argue that no analysis is truly objective—even Frequentists use "Subjective" assumptions (like assuming a Normal distribution). A Prior just makes your assumptions explicit.

1. Types of Priors

Non-Informative (Flat)

When you have no clue. You spread probability equally across all possibilities.

Example: A Uniform distribution \(U(0, 1)\).

Informative

Based on previous experiments, physics, or expert opinion. It has a clear shape (a "peak").

Example: "I'm 90% sure the conversion rate is between 2% and 5%."

Weakly Informative

Ensures parameters stay in a "reasonable" range (e.g., weights shouldn't be 10 million).

The Data Scientist's Choice.

2. Priors and Data Volume

The impact of a Prior fades as you get more data.

  • Small Data (\(n=5\)): The Prior dominates. If your prior is wrong, your results will be wrong.
  • Big Data (\(n=1,000,000\)): The Data dominates. Even a strong prior gets "washed out" by the sheer weight of evidence.

3. ML Connection: Weight Initialization

Deep Learning Backprop

Initialization is a Prior

When you initialize Neural Network weights using Xavier/Glorot or He Initialization, you are setting a Prior.

- Frequentist view: "I'm just picking a random starting point for optimization." - Bayesian view: "I am assuming that weights follow a Normal distribution \(\mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2)\). I believe that big, exploding weights are fundamentally unlikely before I see the training data."

If you set all weights to zero, your "Prior" is a single spike at 0. This makes the network impossible to train (symmetry problem). Choosing a good range for initialization is effectively choosing a Weakly Informative Prior that allows learning to happen efficiently.
