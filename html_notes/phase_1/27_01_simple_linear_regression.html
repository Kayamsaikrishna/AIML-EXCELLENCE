Stats 1.7 Linearity

Simple Linear Regression (SLR)

Finding the "Line of Best Fit" to describe the relationship between one input and one output.

The Identity of Regression

Regression asks a simple question: "How much does Y change if X moves by one unit?"

It is the foundation of all supervised learning. Every Neural Network is just thousands of regressions stacked on top of each other.

1. The Regression Equation

$$ Y = \beta_0 + \beta_1 X + \epsilon $$

Where: - \(Y\): Dependent Variable (Target). - \(X\): Independent Variable (Feature). - \(\beta_0\): Intercept (Where the line hits the Y-axis). - \(\beta_1\): Slope (The "weight" or "impact" of X). - \(\epsilon\): Error (The noise/residuals that the line can't explain).

2. Interpretation of Coefficients

The Intercept (\(\beta_0\))

The value of Y when X is zero. Useful in some contexts (e.g., base salary), but sometimes nonsensical (e.g., height of a person with zero age).

The Slope (\(\beta_1\))

The change in Y for a 1-unit increase in X. This is the Signal we care about in Machine Learning.

3. ML Connection: The Simplest Model

Supervised Interpretability

The Baseline Standard

Before you use a Transformer or a Gradient Boosted Tree, you should ALWAYS run a Simple Linear Regression.

- Benchmark: If a single line gives you 80% accuracy, is it worth adding the complexity of a Neural Network for the extra 2%? - Communication: It's easy to explain to a manager: "For every $1 spent on ads, we get $4 in revenue." You can't say that easily with a Black Box model.

In many industries (Insurance, Finance), "Simple" is often better than "Accurate" because of legal requirements for Explainable AI (XAI).
