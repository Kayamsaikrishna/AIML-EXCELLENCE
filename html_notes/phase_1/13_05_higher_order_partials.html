Calculus 1.6 Curvature

Higher-Order Partial Derivatives

Measuring the twist and turn of space.

Second Derivatives in 3D

Just as \( f''(x) \) measures concavity (curvature), the second partial derivatives measure the curvature of a surface. There are four second-order derivatives for \( f(x, y) \):

  • \( f_{xx} \): Curvature along x-axis.
  • \( f_{yy} \): Curvature along y-axis.
  • \( f_{xy} \) and \( f_{yx} \): Mixed Partials. How the slope in x changes as you move in y.

Clairaut's Theorem: If the functions are continuous, \( f_{xy} = f_{yx} \). The order of differentiation doesn't matter (usually).

2. AI/ML Connection: The Hessian Matrix

The Shape of the Valley

The collection of all second-order derivatives is called the Hessian Matrix (\(H\)). \[ H = \begin{bmatrix} f_{xx} & f_{xy} \\ f_{yx} & f_{yy} \end{bmatrix} \]

The Eigenvalues of the Hessian tell us the geometry of the loss landscape:

  • Both Positive: A Bowl (Local Minimum) -> Good!
  • Both Negative: A Hill (Local Maximum) -> Bad.
  • Mixed Signs: A Saddle Point. This is the nemesis of Gradient Descent. The optimizer gets stuck because it looks like a minimum in one direction but a maximum in another.

3. Python: Computing the Hessian

hessian_matrix.py
import sympy as sp

x, y = sp.symbols('x y')
# Function with a Saddle Point at (0,0): f = x^2 - y^2
f = x**2 - y**2

# First Derivatives (Gradient)
fx = sp.diff(f, x)
fy = sp.diff(f, y)

# Second Derivatives (Hessian)
fxx = sp.diff(fx, x)
fxy = sp.diff(fx, y)
fyx = sp.diff(fy, x)
fyy = sp.diff(fy, y)

hessian = sp.Matrix([[fxx, fxy], [fyx, fyy]])
print("Hessian Matrix:")
sp.pprint(hessian)

# Eigenvalues
eigenvals = hessian.eigenvals()
print(f"\nEigenvalues: {eigenvals}")
# One positive (2), one negative (-2). Confirms Saddle Point.
