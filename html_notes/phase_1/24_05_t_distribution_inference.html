Stats 1.4 Fat Tails

The T-Distribution (Student's T)

Why small datasets need "cautious" distributions that respect the unknown.

The Origin Story

William Sealy Gosset, a chemist at Guinness Brewery, developed this distribution to handle small samples of beer ingredients. Since Guinness didn't allow employees to publish, he used the pseudonym "Student".

1. Heavy Tails vs. Normal

The T-distribution looks like the Normal distribution but has higher probability in the tails.

  • Normal: 95% of data is within ~2 standard deviations.
  • T (df=2): To capture 95% of the data, you have to go out ~4.3 standard deviations!

The Intuition

With only 3 data points, you haven't seen enough to be sure that extreme values are rare. The T-distribution "hedges its bets" by making those extreme values more likely than the Normal distribution would suggest.

2. Convergence

graph TD A["T (df=1) - Very Fat Tails"] --> B["T (df=10)"] B --> C["T (df=30)"] C --> D["Standard Normal (Z)"]

This is why the "Rule of 30" exists in intro stats—at \(n=30\), the T-distribution is practically indistinguishable from the Z-distribution.

3. ML Connection: Robustness

Robustness Deep Learning

Combating Outliers

In many Bayesian ML models, we replace the Gaussian (Normal) likelihood with a **Student-T likelihood**.

Because the T-distribution has heavy tails, it is less "surprised" by outliers. A single extreme data point that would pull a Gaussian mean wildly off track will have less impact on a T-distribution model. This makes the model more Robust.
