15. Chi-Square Goodness-of-Fit

Does reality match the model?

1. The Distribution Match

While the Test of Independence compares two variables, the Goodness-of-Fit test compares our sample frequencies to a **Predefined Theoretical Distribution**. For example, "Is this die fair?" or "Is the distribution of customer types actually 50% Basic, 30% Pro, and 20% Enterprise?".

2. The Mechanism

Just like the independence test, we use $(O-E)^2/E$. But here, the "Expected" values ($E$) come from our theoretical model, not from the sample's own marginals. If the discrepancy is high, we reject the claim that the data fits that distribution.

3. AI/ML Connection: Fairness Audits and OOD Detection

The Ethics of Distribution

In AI, Goodness-of-Fit is the key to Bias Detection and AI Safety.

If you are building an **AI Loan Approver**, you run a **Goodness-of-Fit** test on its outputs. If the population is 50/50 male/female, but the AI approves 80/20, your "Observed" frequencies fail the Goodness-of-Fit test against the "Ethical Equality Distribution." This is how we mathematically **Audit** AI for fairness. In Auto-Monitoring**, we use this test for **Out-of-Distribution (OOD) Detection**. If our model expects "Uniform" input noise but suddenly sees "Poisson" spikes, the Goodness-of-Fit p-value will dive to zero, signaling that the environment has changed and the model needs to be re-trained or shut down before it makes "Confidently Wrong" predictions.

4. Python: stats.chisquare

# Observed counts in 5 categories
obs = [18, 22, 16, 25, 19]

# Theoretical Expected (Assuming Uniform: 100/5 = 20 each)
exp = [20, 20, 20, 20, 20]

stat, p = stats.chisquare(obs, f_exp=exp)

print(f"p-value: {p:.4f}")
# If p > 0.05, we have NO reason to doubt the uniform distribution.
