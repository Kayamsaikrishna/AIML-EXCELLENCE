Stats 1.7 Feature Interaction

Multicollinearity

When your features are "talking over each other". If two inputs tell the same story, the model loses its mind.

The Redundant Roommate

Imagine you ask two friends for the time. One says "10:00" and the other says "10:00". You have twice as much "input" but zero new "information".

In regression, this causes the Variance of Coefficients to explode. The weights (\(\beta\)) become incredibly sensitive to tiny changes in data.

1. Detection: Variance Inflation Factor (VIF)

$$ VIF_j = \frac{1}{1 - R^2_j} $$

Where \(R^2_j\) is the R-squared obtained by regressing feature \(j\) against **all other features**.

VIF = 1

No correlation. This feature is a "Lone Wolf". Perfect.

VIF > 5 or 10

High multicollinearity. This feature is redundant. It's time to delete it or merge it.

2. The Consequences

Wobbly Weights

You might see a feature that should be positive (like House Size) suddenly get a negative coefficient just because it's correlated with Number of Rooms.

Inference Failure

You can't trust p-values. A feature might seem "insignificant" simply because its partner is stealing the spotlight, even though the feature itself is very important.

3. ML Connection: PCA and Regularization

Deep Learning Feature Eng

The Engineering Fix

In big data pipelines, we usually solve multicollinearity via:

  • PCA (Principal Component Analysis): Merge correlated features into orthogonal (uncorrelated) components. This is the "Gold Standard" for high-dimensional data.
  • Ridge Regression (L2): Specifically designed to handle multicollinearity by shrinking weights instead of picking "winners" and "losers".
  • Drop high-VIF features: Simple, effective, and keeps the model interpretable.
Neural Network Tip: While collinearity confuses a linear regression, deep networks are more robust to it because they can learn non-linear combinations. However, it still slows down convergence and increases memory usage!
