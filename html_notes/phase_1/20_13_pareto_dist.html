Prob 1.4 The 80/20 Rule

The Pareto Distribution

Modeling extreme inequality: The world of Power Laws.

The Vital Few

In 1896, Vilfredo Pareto observed that 80% of the land in Italy was owned by 20% of the population. This "Predictable Inequality" turned out to be a universal principle.

"20% of features drive 80% of model performance."
"20% of bugs cause 80% of crashes."

1. Definition and PDF

\(X \sim \text{Pareto}(x_m, \alpha)\) where \(x_m\) is the minimum possible value and \(\alpha\) is the tail index (shape). $$ f(x) = \frac{\alpha x_m^\alpha}{x^{\alpha+1}} $$ for \(x \ge x_m\).
  • The PDF drops off as a power of \(x\) (\(x^\alpha\)), not exponentially. This means the tail is very heavy.
  • Extremely high values are rare, but significantly more likely than in a Normal or Log-Normal distribution.

2. The Hierarchy of Means

Depending on \(\alpha\), the Pareto distribution starts "breaking":

  • If \(\alpha \le 1\): The distribution has Infinite Mean.
  • If \(\alpha \le 2\): The distribution has Infinite Variance.

Wealth distributions often have \(\alpha \approx 1.1\), meaning they are so unequal that the "average wealth" isn't a stable or meaningful metric for individual prediction.

3. ML Connection: Feature Sparsity & Zipf's Law

NLP Data Mining

Zipf's Law

In Natural Language Processing, the frequency of words follows a power law. The word "the" appears twice as often as the 2nd most frequent word, three times as often as the 3rd, and so on.

Implication: Word frequency datasets are extremely unbalanced. Most of your vocabulary will appear only once or twice (the "Long Tail"). This is why we use techniques like Sub-word Tokenization (BPE) or TF-IDF—to stop the most frequent terms from drowning out the rare, informative ones.

Customer Value (CLV)

In E-commerce, customer spending often follows a Pareto distribution. A tiny fraction of "Whales" or super-users contribute the vast majority of revenue. Marketing models must identify these outliers correctly.

4. Scale Invariance

Pareto distributions are self-similar. If you look at the top 1% of wealth owners, the distribution of wealth within that 1% is also a Pareto distribution with the same shape!
