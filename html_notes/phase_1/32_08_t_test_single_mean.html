08. t-test for a Single Mean

The realistic approach to small-sample inference.

1. The Reality Check

In most research, we do not know the population standard deviation ($\sigma$). Instead, we have to estimate it from our sample ($s$). This estimation adds extra "Uncertainty" to the model. We compensate for this by using the **t-distribution** instead of the Normal distribution.

2. The formula & Degrees of Freedom

$$ t = \frac{\bar{x} - \mu_0}{s / \sqrt{n}} $$

The t-distribution depends on **Degrees of Freedom ($df = n - 1$)**. As $df$ increases, the t-distribution becomes identical to the Normal distribution. For small samples, the t-distribution has "Fatter Tails," making it harder to reject the null hypothesis—this is the mathematical "Penalty" for not knowing the true population spread.

3. AI/ML Connection: Fine-tuning and Small-Scale Validation

Is the 1% worth it?

In AI, the t-test is used for Incremental Improvement Verification.

When you are **Fine-tuning a Large Language Model (LLM)** on a specific domain (like legal or medical text), you might only have 10-15 "Gold Standard" evaluation prompts. Because your test set is so small ($n=12$), a Z-test is invalid and dangerous. You use a **One-sample t-test** to determine if the LLM's average accuracy (say, 82%) is significantly higher than the baseline (75%). The t-test's "Heavier Tails" ensure that you don't over-interpret a "Lucky" streak in your tiny test set. It provides a conservative, scientific hurdle that prevents engineers from making grandiose claims about "Breakthroughs" that are actually just sampling noise.

4. Python: scipy.stats.ttest_1samp

# Accuracy of a model on 15 test cases
scores = [0.72, 0.75, 0.68, 0.81, 0.77, 0.73, 0.70, 0.69, 0.74, 0.76, 0.72, 0.75]

# Baseline we want to beat: 0.70
baseline = 0.70

t_stat, p_val = stats.ttest_1samp(scores, baseline)

print(f"t-statistic: {t_stat:.2f}, p-value: {p_val:.4f}")
# If p < 0.05, we have statistically significant proof 
# that the fine-tuned model is better than the baseline.
