13. Interaction Terms

When the effect of one variable depends on another.

1. Synergy and Interference

Standard regression assumes variables act independently. But often, the effect of $X_1$ on $Y$ changes based on the value of $X_2$. We model this with an **Interaction Term** ($X_1 \times X_2$):

$$ Y = \beta_0 + \beta_1 X_1 + \beta_2 X_2 + \beta_3 (X_1 \times X_2) + \epsilon $$

Example: "Rain" alone might not affect traffic much, and "Accidents" alone might not either, but "Rain" AND "Accidents" together cause total gridlock.

2. Hierarchical Principle

If you include an interaction term $X_1 \times X_2$, you MUST also include the raw terms $X_1$ and $X_2$, even if their individual p-values are not significant. This ensures the model is logically consistent.

3. AI/ML Connection: Feature Crosses and High-order Dependencies

Solving the XOR Problem

In AI, Interaction terms are known as Feature Crosses**.

A simple linear model cannot solve the **XOR problem** because it acts variable-by-variable. By adding an interaction term, you "Linearize" the non-linearity. In Recommendation Engines (DeepFM / Wide & Deep)**, feature crosses like 'UserAge' $\times$ 'MovieGenre' are the most powerful signals. They capture Hyper-Personalization**. While Deep Neural Networks "Implicitly" discover these crosses through layers, explicitly defining interaction terms in your regression baseline makes the model **Lightweight** and **Fast enough for real-time mobile apps**. Understanding interactions is the key to understanding why "Adding more data" isn't as effective as "Capturing more relationships."

4. Python: Creating Interaction Terms

# Using sklearn to create the cross-product
poly = PolynomialFeatures(degree=2, interaction_only=True)
X_interact = poly.fit_transform(X)

# Feature names will include 'x0', 'x1', and 'x0*x1'
model = LinearRegression().fit(X_interact, y)
