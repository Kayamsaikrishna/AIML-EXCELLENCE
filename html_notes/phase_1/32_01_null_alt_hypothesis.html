01. Null and Alternative Hypotheses

The Logic of "Innocent until proven guilty."

1. The Foundation

Every statistical test begins with two competing statements about a population parameter:

  • Null Hypothesis ($H_0$): The statement being tested. It usually represents "No effect," "No difference," or "Status Quo." We assume $H_0$ is true unless our data provides strong evidence against it.
  • Alternative Hypothesis ($H_1$ or $H_a$): The statement we want to support. it represents a change, an effect, or a difference.

2. The Courtroom Analogy

In a trial, the defendant is presumed innocent ($H_0$) until proven guilty ($H_1$). The "Burden of Proof" lies with the prosecution (the data). If the evidence is not strong enough, we **Fail to Reject $H_0$**—we don't say the defendant is "Innocent," only that we don't have enough proof to convict.

3. AI/ML Connection: Model Sanity Checking

Is the AI actually learning?

In AI research, the $H_0$ is your most important benchmark.

When you build a new recommendation engine, your $H_0$ is "The new AI has the same click-through rate as the old one." If you can't reject $H_0$, you shouldn't ship the model. In Anomaly Detection**, the $H_0$ is "This input data is normal." When an AI flags a fraudulent transaction, it is essentially saying: "The evidence against $H_0$ is so strong that I am rejecting normalcy in favor of $H_1$ (Fraud)." Without this logical framework, AI would just be "Pattern matching" without any concept of **False Alarms** or **Evidence thresholds**, leading to systems that are untrustworthy in high-stakes environments.

4. Python: Defining Hypotheses

# Scenario: Testing if a coin is biased.
# H0: p = 0.5 (Fair)
# H1: p != 0.5 (Biased)

true_p = 0.55 # Unknown to the test
obs_heads = np.random.binomial(100, true_p)

# We start by ASSUMING H0:
p_null = 0.5
expected_heads = 100 * p_null

print(f"Observed: {obs_heads}, Expected under H0: {expected_heads}")
# If |obs - exp| is "Large", we will question H0.
