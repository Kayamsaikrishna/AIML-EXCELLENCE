13. ARIMA Models

The classical gold standard of time-series forecasting.

1. The (p, d, q) Mastermind

ARIMA (AutoRegressive Integrated Moving Average)** is a unified framework for modeling time series data. It has three core parts:

  • AR (p) - AutoRegressive: Relationship between an observation and some number of lagged observations. ($Y_t$ depends on $Y_{t-1}, \dots, Y_{t-p}$).
  • I (d) - Integrated: Number of times the data must be differenced to become stationary.
  • MA (q) - Moving Average: Relationship between an observation and a residual error from a moving average model applied to lagged observations.

2. The Equation

$$ \hat{y}_t = \mu + \phi_1 y_{t-1} + \dots + \phi_p y_{t-p} + \theta_1 \epsilon_{t-1} + \dots + \theta_q \epsilon_{t-q} + \epsilon_t $$

It essentially treats every time step as a linear regression of its own past + the error of its previous guesses.

3. AI/ML Connection: The Baseline for Sequence Learning

Linear Recurrence

In AI, ARIMA is the Recurrent Neural Network's Linear Ancestor**.

An **AR(1) model** ($y_t = \phi y_{t-1}$) is exactly what happens inside a single **RNN cell** without the non-linear activation. Before you use an **LSTM** or **GRU**, you run an ARIMA model to see the "Linear Baseline." If ARIMA gets a 5% error, and your complex Deep Learning model gets a 4.5% error, the extra cost of GPUs might not be worth the complexity. ARIMA is also used in Ensemble Models (e.g., DeepAR)** where the AI handles the "Big Data" part and ARIMA handles the "Global Linear Trend" part. It provides the **Statistical Guarantees** (Confidence Intervals) that pure Neural Networks often lack. Understanding (p,d,q) is the key to understanding **Backpropagation-through-time (BPTT)** on its most fundamental level.

4. Python: Fitting an ARIMA

from statsmodels.tsa.arima.model import ARIMA

# Fit an ARIMA(1, 1, 1) model
model = ARIMA(data, order=(1, 1, 1))
results = model.fit()

print(results.summary())
# The 'coef' values are the Phi and Theta from the math!
