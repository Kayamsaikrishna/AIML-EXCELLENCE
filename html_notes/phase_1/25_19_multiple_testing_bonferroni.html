Stats 1.5 Alpha Inflation

Multiple Testing Correction

Guarding against the "look-elsewhere effect" where you find a discovery just by asking too many questions.

The Problem: Alpha Inflation

If you test one hypothesis at \(\alpha = 0.05\), you have a 5% chance of a False Positive.
If you test 100 hypotheses, your chance of at least ONE False Positive is:
\(1 - (0.95)^{100} = \mathbf{99.4\%}\)

Discovery is almost guaranteed, but it's pure garbage!

1. The Bonferroni Correction

$$ \alpha_{new} = \frac{\alpha_{old}}{m} $$
  • \(m\): Total number of tests performed.

If you conduct 20 tests, your new significance threshold is \(0.05 / 20 = \mathbf{0.0025}\). You now require much stronger evidence for any single discovery to be counted.

2. Pros and Cons

Pros

  • Very simple to calculate.
  • Extremely conservative (strictly controls Type I error).
  • The "Gold Standard" for medical trials.

Cons

  • Low Power: It makes it so hard to "Reject" that you often miss real discoveries.
  • Assumes tests are independent (rarely true in ML features).

3. ML Connection: Feature Selection

Supervised Genomics

Curse of Categorical Features

In Bioinformatics, we test 20,000 genes to see which ones cause cancer.

Without Bonferroni, we would find 1,000 genes "Significantly" linked to cancer by pure chance (\(20,000 \times 0.05\)). These are False Discoveries!

By applying Bonferroni (\(0.05 / 20000\)), we ensure that the genes we pick are actually meaningful. In generic ML, if you are doing Univariate Feature Selection on 500 features, you MUST apply a correction, otherwise your model will be built on ghosts.
