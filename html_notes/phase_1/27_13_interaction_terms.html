Stats 1.7 Feature Interaction

Interaction Terms

Modeling "It Depends". When the impact of Feature A changes based on the value of Feature B.

The Synergy Effect

Does "Exercise" make you lose weight? It depends on your "Diet".

If we model them separately, we miss the magic. An interaction term (\(X_1 \times X_2\)) captures the synergy where the combination is greater (or less) than the sum of its parts.

1. The Equation

$$ Y = \beta_0 + \beta_1 X_1 + \beta_2 X_2 + \beta_3 (X_1 \times X_2) + \epsilon $$

- \(\beta_1\): The "Main Effect" of \(X_1\). - \(\beta_3\): The "Interaction Effect". It tells us how the slope of \(X_1\) changes as \(X_2\) changes.

2. Common Scenarios

Condition x Continuous

Is the effect of "Education" on "Salary" different for "Men" vs "Women"?
Here, Gender (Binary) interacts with Years of Schooling (Continuous).

Continuous x Continuous

Advertising on "TV" and "Radio". Does spending on Radio make TV ads more effective? (The Synergy Effect).

3. ML Connection: Cross-Features

Deep Learning Ads/Recs

The Power of Cross-Products

In modern Recommender Systems (like YouTube or Amazon), interaction terms are the "Secret Sauce".

  • Recommender Systems: Simply knowing a user likes "Action Movies" and "Tom Cruise" isn't enough. The model needs an interaction term `User_Likes_Action * Actor_is_Tom_Cruise` to represent that specific preference.
  • Wide & Deep Models: Google famously uses "Wide" parts (Interaction terms) to memorize specific feature combinations and "Deep" parts (Neural Networks) to generalize.
  • Decision Trees: Random Forests and XGBoost are Interaction Machines. A tree split on X after a split on Y is naturally modeling an interaction. Linear regression needs us to manually create these (\(X \times Y\)), but Trees find them automatically.
Warning: If you add an interaction term (\(X_1 \times X_2\)), you MUST include the individual terms (\(X_1\) and \(X_2\)) as well, even if they aren't significant. This is the Hierarchical Principle.
