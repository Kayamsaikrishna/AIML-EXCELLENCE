16. Conditional Gaussian Distributions

Updating high-dimensional beliefs with new evidence.

1. The Partition View

If we have a Gaussian vector $\mathbf{x}$ partitioned into two sets of variables, $\mathbf{x}_A$ and $\mathbf{x}_B$, then the distribution of $\mathbf{x}_A$ given $\mathbf{x}_B = \mathbf{b}$ is also a Gaussian Distribution.

$$ \boldsymbol{\mu}_{A|B} = \boldsymbol{\mu}_A + \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{AB} \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{BB}^{-1} (\mathbf{b} - \boldsymbol{\mu}_B) $$ $$ \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{A|B} = \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{AA} - \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{AB} \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{BB}^{-1} \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{BA} $$

2. Intuition: Information Gain

The conditional mean $\boldsymbol{\mu}_{A|B}$ shows how our "best guess" for $A$ shifts based on the evidence $B$. The conditional covariance $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{A|B}$ (the **Schur Complement**) represents the remaining uncertainty; it is always "smaller" (or equal) than the original uncertainty $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{AA}$.

3. AI/ML Connection: Sensor Fusion and Forecasting

Connecting the Dots

Conditional Gaussians are why autonomous systems can Predict the Invisible.

In **Kalman Filters**, used in self-driving cars and satellites, we have a Gaussian belief about where the car is. We get a "noisy" GPS signal (evidence $\mathbf{x}_B$). By calculating the conditional Gaussian, the AI updates its position estimate ($\boldsymbol{\mu}_{A|B}$) and narrows down its uncertainty ($\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{A|B}$). In **Gaussian Processes**, we model an entire function as a Gaussian. When the AI sees a few data points, it uses conditional Gaussian math to predict the entire curve and provide a confidence interval for every point on it. Understanding this math is the difference between an AI that "guesses" and one that "calculates its own certainty."

4. Python: Calculating Conditional Beliefs

import numpy as np

# Partitioned Mean and Covariance
mu_a, mu_b = 10, 20
s_aa, s_bb, s_ab = 1.0, 4.0, 0.8 # Variance A, Variance B, Cov(A,B)

# If we observe evidence B = 22
b_obs = 22

# Updated Mean of A
mu_cond = mu_a + (s_ab / s_bb) * (b_obs - mu_b)
# Updated Variance of A
var_cond = s_aa - (s_ab**2 / s_bb)

print(f"Original A: {mu_a} +/- {np.sqrt(s_aa):.2f}")
print(f"Updated A: {mu_cond:.2f} +/- {np.sqrt(var_cond):.2f}")
