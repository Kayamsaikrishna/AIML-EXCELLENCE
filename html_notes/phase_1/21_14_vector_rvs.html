Prob 1.4 The Feature Vector

Vector Random Variables

Scaling probability theory to handle arrays of variables simultaneously.

Thinking in Tensors

In ML, we don't process numbers one by one. We process Feature Verticals (columns) and Sample Vectors (rows).

A Random Vector \(\mathbf{X}\) is simply a collection of random variables mapped into a single mathematical object. This allows us to use Linear Algebra to solve probability problems.

1. Notation

A random vector \(\mathbf{X}\) in \(\mathbb{R}^n\) is: $$ \mathbf{X} = \begin{bmatrix} X_1 \\ X_2 \\ \vdots \\ X_n \end{bmatrix} $$ Its Mean Vector is: $$ E[\mathbf{X}] = \mu = \begin{bmatrix} E[X_1] \\ E[X_2] \\ \vdots \\ E[X_n] \end{bmatrix} $$

2. Linear Transformations

If \(\mathbf{Y} = \mathbf{A}\mathbf{X} + \mathbf{b}\), then:
  • \(E[\mathbf{Y}] = \mathbf{A}E[\mathbf{X}] + \mathbf{b}\)
  • \(\Sigma_{\mathbf{Y}} = \mathbf{A} \Sigma_{\mathbf{X}} \mathbf{A}^\top\)

Why is the transpose there? The covariance matrix is a 2nd-order object. When you multiply a vector by a matrix \(\mathbf{A}\), its variance (or "spread volume") scales by the squared magnitude of the transformation. \(\mathbf{A}\dots\mathbf{A}^\top\) is the matrix version of squaring.

3. ML Connection: Neural Network Activations

Deep Learning Activations

Propagating Uncertainty

Every layer in a Neural Network is a transformation of a random vector: $$ \mathbf{h} = \sigma(\mathbf{W}\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{b}) $$ To prevent Vanishing or Exploding Gradients, we need to ensure that the variance of the random vector \(\mathbf{h}\) stays within a reasonable range throughout the deep network.

This is the core motivation behind Xavier/He Initialization and Batch Normalization—carefully controlling the mean and covariance of these high-dimensional hidden vectors.

4. Dot Products and Projections

When we take a dot product \(\mathbf{w}^\top\mathbf{x}\) (a single neuron's calculation), we are projecting a multi-dimensional random vector onto a 1D scalar line.
The variance of this scalar is: $$ \text{Var}(\mathbf{w}^\top\mathbf{x}) = \mathbf{w}^\top \Sigma_{\mathbf{x}} \mathbf{w} $$
