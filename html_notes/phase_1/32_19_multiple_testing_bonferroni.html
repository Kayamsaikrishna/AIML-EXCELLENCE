19. Multiple Testing Correction: Bonferroni

Fighting the "p-hacking" monster in large-scale experiments.

1. The Problem of Multiple Comparisons

If you perform 100 independent tests with $\alpha=0.05$, you are **Guaranteed** to find about 5 "Statistically Significant" results purely by luck, even if there is no real effect. This is called the Multiplicity Problem. In AI, where we test thousands of configurations, this is a massive source of "Fooling ourselves."

2. The Bonferroni Solution

The simplest way to control the **Family-Wise Error Rate (FWER)** is to divide your alpha by the number of tests ($m$).

$$ \alpha_{new} = \frac{\alpha_{original}}{m} $$

If you run 100 tests, your new significance threshold is $0.05 / 100 = 0.0005$. It is extremely conservative and very hard to pass, but it ensures that the probability of *even one* false positive is kept below 5%.

3. AI/ML Connection: Grid Search and Data Mining

Is it a breakthrough or a fluke?

In AI, Bonferroni is use for High-Confidence Experimentation.

When you run a **Large-Scale Grid Search** for an architecture, testing 1,000 different combinations of dropout, layers, and gates, you *will* find one that looks amazing by chance. If your best model has $p=0.01$, but you ran 1,000 tests, its "corrected" p-value is effectively $0.01 \times 1,000 = 10.0$ (Not significant!). By using Bonferroni, AI Researchers ensure that they don't publish "Fluke" architectures that fail to replicate on other datasets. In Bioinformatics AI**, where we test 20,000 genes to find markers for cancer, Bonferroni is the "Shield" that prevents us from telling patients they have a genetic risk when the AI just saw a random pattern in the noise.

4. Python: statsmodels.stats.multitest

from statsmodels.stats.multitest import multipletests

# Results from 10 different experiments
p_vals = [0.001, 0.01, 0.045, 0.12, 0.45, 0.0001, 0.02, 0.8, 0.9, 0.03]

# Apply Bonferroni correction
reject, pvals_corrected, _, _ = multipletests(p_vals, alpha=0.05, method='bonferroni')

print(f"Significant results after correction: {sum(reject)}")
# Originally 5 were p < 0.05. After correction, only 2 remain!
