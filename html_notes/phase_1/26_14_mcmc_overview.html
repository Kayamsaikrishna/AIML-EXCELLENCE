Stats 1.6 Inference Engine

MCMC (Markov Chain Monte Carlo)

Exploring the world of probability by wandering through it, so you don't have to solve impossible integrals.

The Problem of the Denominator

In Bayes' Theorem, the bottom term \(P(D)\) is a multi-dimensional integral that grows exponentially with the number of features. For a model with 1,000 weights, you'd need a 1,000-dimensional integral.

MCMC says: "Forget the integral. I'll just draw a million samples from the posterior. The histogram of those samples IS the answer I'm looking for."

1. The Two Halves of MCMC

1. Monte Carlo

Solving complex problems by repeated random sampling. If you want to know the area of a circle, throw 1,000 darts at a square and count the hits.

2. Markov Chain

A sequence where the next step depends ONLY on the current step. This allows the sampler to "wander" towards high-probability regions and spend more time there.

2. How it works (Simplified)

1. Start at a random location in parameter space.
2. Suggest a "jump" to a nearby location.
3. If the new spot has higher probability, Move there.
4. If the new spot has lower probability, Maybe move there (probabilistically).
5. Repeat thousands of times.

3. ML Connection: Bayesian Neural Networks (BNNs)

Deep Learning Uncertainty

Modeling Weight Clouds

In a standard Neural Network, a weight \(w_{12}\) is a single number (e.g., 0.5).

In a Bayesian NN, that weight is a "cloud" of possible values. To train such a network, we use MCMC (or its faster variants like HMC) to sample these clouds.

When we make a prediction, we don't pass the input through the network once. We pass it through 100 times, each time using a different sample of weights from our MCMC "belief." If the 100 outputs are all consistent, the model is sure. If the 100 outputs are wildly different, the model is effectively saying: "I have no idea what this image is." This is critical for **Medical AI** and Self-Driving Cars.
