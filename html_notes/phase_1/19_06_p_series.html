06. p-Series

Finding the "tipping point" between finite and infinite.

1. The Test

The p-series is a series of the form:

$$ \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{1}{n^p} = 1 + \frac{1}{2^p} + \frac{1}{3^p} + \dots $$

The p-series Test states:

  • If \(p > 1\), the series converges.
  • If \(p \leq 1\), the series diverges.

2. The Harmonic Series

When \(p = 1\), we get the Harmonic Series (\(1 + 1/2 + 1/3 + \dots\)). Even though the terms go to zero, they don't do it fast enough. The sum grows to infinity very slowly. This is the boundary between convergent and divergent behavior.

3. AI/ML Connection: Zipf's Law

The Math of Language

In Natural Language Processing (NLP), we observe that the frequency of words follows Zipf's Law. The most common word appears roughly twice as often as the second most common, three times as often as the third, and so on.

This is a p-series with \(p \approx 1\). This means that if you had an infinite dictionary, the total "Weight" of all words would diverge. This physical reality is why AI models like GPT-4 use a "Vocabulary Limit". If they tried to learn every possible "rare" word, the model's memory requirements would follow a Divergent p-Series, eventually crashing the system. By understanding the p-series, engineers can balance "Model Breadth" with "Finite Memory".

4. Python: Visualizing Divergence

# p=1 (Harmonic) vs p=2 (Convergent)
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

n = list(range(1, 100))
harmonic = [sum(1/i for i in range(1, x+1)) for x in n]
convergent = [sum(1/(i**2) for i in range(1, x+1)) for x in n]

plt.plot(n, harmonic, label="p=1 (Divergent)")
plt.plot(n, convergent, label="p=2 (Convergent)")
plt.legend()
plt.show() # In a real environment, this shows p=2 flattening while p=1 grows.
