Polynomial Regression

Converting a linear engine into a curve-generating machine by treating powers of X as new features.

The Big Secret

Polynomial Regression is still Linear Regression.

Wait, what? Yes! It is linear in the parameters (\(\beta\)), even if it is non-linear in the features (\(X\)). This means we can still use OLS and the Normal Equation to solve it.

1. The Equation

$$ Y = \beta_0 + \beta_1 X + \beta_2 X^2 + \beta_3 X^3 + ... + \epsilon $$

We treat \(X^2\) and \(X^3\) as if they were independent features like "Height" and "Weight".

2. The Degree Dilemma

Degree 1 (Linear)

High Bias: The model is too simple. It misses the curve. Underfitting.

Degree 2-3

The Sweet Spot: Captures real-world patterns (like acceleration or growth decay).

Degree 10+

High Variance: The model wiggles through every single data point. It learns the noise. Overfitting.

3. ML Connection: Feature Expansion

Feature Eng Deep Learning

Why we still care

In scikit-learn, we use `PolynomialFeatures()` to pre-process data. Why?

  • Taylor Series: Almost any smooth curve can be approximated by a polynomial if the degree is high enough. This is how we teach basic linear models to handle complex geometry.
  • Support Vector Machines (SVM): The "Polynomial Kernel" in SVMs is essentially doing this feature expansion in a high-dimensional space without actually calculating the squares (Kernel Trick).
  • Deep Learning: A single neuron with a non-linear activation (like ReLU) is essentially a piece-wise polynomial. Neural networks are essentially massive, flexible polynomial engines.
Warning: Polynomials explode at the edges! While they fit the training data well, their predictions for values outside the original range (Extrapolation) are often wildly wrong.
