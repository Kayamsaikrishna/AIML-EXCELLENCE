Stats 1.5 Directionality

One vs Two-Tailed Tests

Deciding whether you are looking for ANY difference, or a specific improvement.

The Curiosity Limit

A hypothesis test is directional if you only care if one side happens. It is non-directional if you are looking for a change in either direction (increase or decrease).

Two-Tailed (Non-directional)

  • Question: "Is there a difference?"
  • \(H_1: \mu \neq 50\)
  • Rejection region is split into two tails (\(\alpha/2\) each).
  • Harder to reject (conservative).

One-Tailed (Directional)

  • Question: "Is it better?" (or worse?)
  • \(H_1: \mu > 50\) (or \(<\))
  • Rejection region is entirely in one tail.
  • Easier to reject (if you are right about the direction).

1. The Rejection Regions

Because the One-Tailed test puts all it's "doubt quota" into one side, its critical value is lower (e.g., 1.64 for one-tail vs 1.96 for two-tail at 95% confidence).

2. ML Connection: Benchmark Performance

Research Validation

The One-Tailed Trap

When you develop a new model, you usually only care if it's better than the baseline. This sounds like a One-Tailed test (\(H_a: \text{New} > \text{Baseline}\)).

The Danger: If you use a one-tailed test, and your new model is actually worse, the test statistic will be in the wrong tail and you will "Fail to Reject."

Standard Practice: Most scientific journals and ML benchmarks use **Two-Tailed tests** by default. It is more rigorous because it checks for difference in both directions (is it better OR worse?). If you use a one-tailed test just to get a lower P-value, your peers might call it cheating!
