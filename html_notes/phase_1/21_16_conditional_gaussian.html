Conditional Gaussian Distributions

Predicting the unknown based on the known, using the most elegant math in probability.

The Predictive Power of Gaussians

If we have a joint Gaussian distribution over two sets of variables, \(\mathbf{x}_1\) and \(\mathbf{x}_2\), and we observe the value of \(\mathbf{x}_2\), what is our new belief about \(\mathbf{x}_1\)?

The answer is another Gaussian. The mean shifts based on the correlation, and the variance shrinks because we now have more information.

1. The Schur Complement Formula

Let \(\mathbf{x} = [\mathbf{x}_1, \mathbf{x}_2]^\top \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu, \Sigma)\) with: $$ \Sigma = \begin{bmatrix} \Sigma_{11} & \Sigma_{12} \\ \Sigma_{21} & \Sigma_{22} \end{bmatrix} $$ The conditional distribution \(P(\mathbf{x}_1 \mid \mathbf{x}_2)\) is Gaussian with:
  • Mean: \(\mu_{1|2} = \mu_1 + \Sigma_{12}\Sigma_{22}^{-1}(\mathbf{x}_2 - \mu_2)\)
  • Covariance: \(\Sigma_{1|2} = \Sigma_{11} - \Sigma_{12}\Sigma_{22}^{-1}\Sigma_{21}\)

2. Intuition

  • The Mean Shift: If \(X_1\) and \(X_2\) are highly correlated, seeing a large values of \(X_2\) "drags" the expected value of \(X_1\) higher.
  • The Variance Reduction: Notice that \(\Sigma_{1|2}\) is the original variance minus a term. Knowledge of \(X_2\) always makes our prediction of \(X_1\) more certain (or at worst, doesn't change it).

3. ML Connection: Kalman Filters & Bayesian Regression

Robotics Time Series

Kalman Filters

A Kalman filter tracks the state of a system (like a self-driving car) over time. Every time we get a sensor reading (observation), we perform a Conditional Gaussian update.

- Prediction: Project the previous state forward (Normal distribution).
- Correction: Condition the prediction on the new measurement. The formulas above are exactly how a car "merges" its internal movement model with its GPS readings to find its precise location.

Bayesian Linear Regression

In Bayesian regression, we treat the weight vector \(\mathbf{w}\) as a random variable. After seeing training data, we compute the conditional distribution of the weights given the data. This allows the model to say "I think the slope is 2.5, but I'm ±0.4 certain."
