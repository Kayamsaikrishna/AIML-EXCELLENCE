KL Divergence Definition

Measuring the Relative Entropy. How much information is "lost" when we approximate a complex truth with a simple model?

The Cost of Approximation

In Science, we rarely know the "True" probability distribution \(P\). We create a "Model" distribution \(Q\).

Kullback-Leibler (KL) Divergence is the measure of the extra bits we have to use because our model \(Q\) is slightly different from the reality \(P\).

1. Definition

For two discrete probability distributions \(P\) and \(Q\):

$$ D_{KL}(P \| Q) = \sum_{x \in \mathcal{X}} P(x) \log_2 \left( \frac{P(x)}{Q(x)} \right) $$

Equivalently, it is the difference between Cross-Entropy and Entropy:

$$ D_{KL}(P \| Q) = H(P, Q) - H(P) $$
The Zero Rule: For the sum to be finite, \(Q(x)\) must be non-zero whenever \(P(x)\) is non-zero. If your model says something is impossible (\(Q=0\)) but it actually happens (\(P>0\)), your KL Divergence is Infinite.

2. Asymmetry: The "Forward" vs "Reverse" KL

One of the most confusing (and important) facts about KL is that it is not a true "distance" because it is not symmetric.

Forward KL (\(D_{KL}(P \| Q)\))

Used in Maximum Likelihood. It forces \(Q\) to cover the entire support of \(P\). If \(P\) is multi-modal, \(Q\) will stretch to cover all peaks (Mean-Seeking).

Reverse KL (\(D_{KL}(Q \| P)\))

Used in Variational Inference. It forces \(Q\) to fit into one of the peaks of \(P\). It avoids placing probability mass where \(P\) is zero (Mode-Seeking).

KL Divergence in Action

  • Variational Autoencoders: We use the "Reverse KL" to force the latent space of our images to follow a standard Unit Gaussian. This makes the latent space smooth enough that we can "walk" through it to generate new images.
  • Knowledge Distillation: We train a small "Student" model to minimize the KL divergence between its outputs and the outputs of a giant "Teacher" model. The student tries to copy the teacher's probability distributions perfectly.
  • t-SNE: This dimensionality reduction tool works by minimizing the KL Divergence between the high-dimensional neighborhood distribution and a low-dimensional map.
