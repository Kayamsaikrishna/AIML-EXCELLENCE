10. Slutsky's Theorem

Applying algebra to probabilistic limits.

1. Definition

Slutsky's Theorem allows us to perform algebraic operations on sequences of random variables that converge in different ways. If $X_n \xrightarrow{d} X$ (Convergence in Distribution) and $Y_n \xrightarrow{p} c$ (Convergence in Probability to a constant), then:

  • $X_n + Y_n \xrightarrow{d} X + c$
  • $X_n Y_n \xrightarrow{d} cX$
  • $X_n / Y_n \xrightarrow{d} X / c$ (if $c \neq 0$)

2. Why It Matters

In many real-world problems, we have one complex part of a formula that slowly becomes Gaussian ($X_n$) and another part that steadily reveals its true value ($Y_n$). Slutsky's Theorem proves that we can "substitute" the constant value into the formula without breaking the convergence of the Gaussian part. This is the "Glue" that holds large-sample statistics together.

3. AI/ML Connection: Hypothesis Testing and T-Tests

The Math of Confidence Intervals

Slutsky's Theorem is why we can use the **Standard Deviation of a Sample** instead of the unknown Population Standard Deviation.

In **Statistics for AI**, when we perform a Z-test or t-test, we calculate a score like $Z = \frac{\bar{X} - \mu}{s / \sqrt{n}}$. Here, the numerator $\bar{X} - \mu$ converges in distribution to a Normal distribution (by CLT), and the denominator $s$ (sample standard deviation) converges in probability to the true $\sigma$ (by WLLN). Slutsky's Theorem guarantees that the resulting ratio $Z$ actually follows a Normal distribution in the limit. Without this theorem, our confidence intervals for AI accuracy would be mathematically invalid because we don't know the "True" variance of the entire world.

4. Python: Observing Slutsky's Logic

# Xn -> N(0, 1) in distribution
# Yn -> 5 in probability
n = 10000
xn = np.random.normal(0, 1, n)
yn = 5 + (1 / np.sqrt(np.arange(1, n+1))) # Converges to 5

# Combined Result
zn = xn * yn[-1]

print(f"Mean of Combined: {np.mean(zn):.4f}") # Near 0
print(f"Std of Combined: {np.std(zn):.4f}")   # Near 5! (cX)
