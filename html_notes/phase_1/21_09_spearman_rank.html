Prob 1.4 Robust Stats

Spearman Rank Correlation (\(r_s\))

Measuring monotonic relationships: When Pearson fails because the relation isn't a straight line.

Beyond the Straight Line

Pearson correlation assumes a linear (straight line) relationship. But what if doubling \(X\) always increases \(Y\), but by different amounts each time (e.g., \(Y = e^X\))?

Pearon will be less than 1. But the relationship is "perfectly monotonic". **Spearman Rank Correlation** solves this by looking at the ranks (order) of the data rather than the values.

1. The Formula

Spearman's \(\rho\) is simply the Pearson correlation of the ranked variables.
For a sample of size \(n\) with no tied ranks: $$ r_s = 1 - \frac{6 \sum d_i^2}{n(n^2 - 1)} $$ where \(d_i\) is the difference between the rank of \(X\) and the rank of \(Y\) for each point.

2. Two Advantages

1. Capture Non-Linearity

If \(Y = e^X\), Pearson might be 0.88. Spearman will be 1.00 because the order is perfectly preserved.

2. Robust to Outliers

A single extreme outlier can destroy a Pearson coefficient. But in Spearman, that outlier is just assigned the "highest rank" (e.g., rank 100), limiting its disruptive power.

3. ML Connection: Feature Selection & Ranking

EDA Ordinal Data

Categorical / Ordinal Features

If your features are ordinal (e.g., "Low", "Medium", "High"), Pearson is not technically valid because the distance between categories isn't defined. Spearman is the standard tool for measuring the relationship between such ranked categories and your target.

Information Bottleneck

In some cases, Spearman can reveal dependencies that Pearson misses. If Spearman is much higher than Pearson, it's a strong hint that you should try a non-linear model (like a Random Forest or Neural Network) or apply a log-transform to your data to linearize it.
