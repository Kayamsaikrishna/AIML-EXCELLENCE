Prob 1.4 Interactions

Functions of Multiple RVs

What happens when Random Variables interact? Sums, Products, and Maxima.

The Combinatorics of Uncertainty

Single variables are rare. In ML, we deal with vectors of variables. We compute sums (neurons), maximums (MaxPooling), and averages (Ensembles).

The key question: If I know how \(X\) behaves and how \(Y\) behaves, how does \(Z = X+Y\) behave? The answer is often Convolution.

1. The Sum of Independent RVs (Convolution)

If \(Z = X + Y\) where \(X\) and \(Y\) are independent continuous RVs: $$ f_Z(z) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} f_X(x) f_Y(z-x) \, dx $$ This is the Convolution of the two PDFs: \(f_Z = f_X * f_Y\).

Why? To get a sum of \(z\), \(X\) can be any value \(x\), but then \(Y\) must be exactly \(z-x\). We sum (integrate) over all possible \(x\)'s weighting by their probabilities.

  • Sum of Normals: \(\mathcal{N}(\mu_1, \sigma_1^2) + \mathcal{N}(\mu_2, \sigma_2^2) = \mathcal{N}(\mu_1+\mu_2, \sigma_1^2+\sigma_2^2)\). (The bell curve stays bell-shaped).
  • Sum of Uniforms: Uniform + Uniform = Triangular Distribution.

2. Max and Min (Order Statistics)

CNNs MaxPooling

The Geometry of "Max"

In Convolutional Neural Networks, MaxPooling selects the largest activation in a region. If inputs are noisy random variables, what is the distribution of the output?

The Logic

Let \(Z = \max(X, Y)\). For \(Z\) to be less than some value \(z\), BOTH \(X\) and \(Y\) must be less than \(z\).

$$ F_Z(z) = P(Z \le z) = P(X \le z \text{ AND } Y \le z) $$

If independent:

$$ F_{max}(z) = F_X(z) \cdot F_Y(z) $$

Similarly for Minimum \(W = \min(X, Y)\):
For \(W > w\), BOTH must be \(> w\).
\(P(W > w) = (1 - F_X(w))(1 - F_Y(w))\).

3. The Ratio Distribution

\(Z = X/Y\). This is often heavy-tailed (Cauchy Distribution).

Caution: Even if \(X\) and \(Y\) are well-behaved Normal variables, their ratio \(X/Y\) (Cauchy) has undefined mean and variance. This is why dividing by learned weights in Neural Networks can be dangerous without numerical stability terms (epsilon).

4. Expectation vs. Function

A classic blunder:

$$ E[g(X)] \neq g(E[X]) $$

(Unless \(g\) is linear). This is Jensen's Inequality.

  • \(E[X^2] \neq (E[X])^2\) (Difference is Variance)
  • \(E[\log(X)] \le \log(E[X])\)

In the EM algorithm (Expectation-Maximization), Jensen's inequality is used to construct a lower bound on the likelihood.
