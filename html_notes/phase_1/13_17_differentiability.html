17. Differentiability in Multiple Variables

Ensuring a smooth, linear approximation exists.

1. The Formal Definition

In multivariable calculus, just having partial derivatives does not mean the function is differentiable. A function is Differentiable at \((a, b)\) if the linear approximation (the tangent plane) becomes perfectly accurate as you zoom in.

Formally, \(f\) is differentiable if \(\Delta z\) can be written as:

$$ \Delta z = f_x(a, b)\Delta x + f_y(a, b)\Delta y + \epsilon_1\Delta x + \epsilon_2\Delta y $$

Where \(\epsilon_1, \epsilon_2 \to 0\) as \((\Delta x, \Delta y) \to (0, 0)\).

2. Continuity of Partials

A sufficient condition: If the partial derivatives \(f_x\) and \(f_y\) exist near \((a, b)\) and are continuous at \((a, b)\), then the function is guaranteed to be differentiable. This is why "Smoothness" is such a big deal in applied math.

3. AI/ML Connection: The Smoothness Assumption

Adam and Momentum Stability

Optimization algorithms like Adam or RMSProp assume that the loss function is locally Differentiable. If we encounter a point that is not differentiable (like a "cusp" or "cliff"), the linear model of the landscape breaks down.

In high dimensions, we use Smooth Activations (like Swish or GELU) instead of ReLU specifically to ensure that the function is differentiable everywhere. This lack of "Mathematical Sharpness" makes the training more stable because the gradient doesn't suddenly vanish or flip direction. Differentiability is the technical requirement for a model to be "Trainable" via standard calculus methods.
