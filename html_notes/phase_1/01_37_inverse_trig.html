37. Inverse Trigonometric Functions

Going from Ratios back to Angles.

1. Domain & Range Restrictions

Trig functions are not one-to-one (they fail the horizontal line test). To have inverses, we must restrict their domains:

$$ y = \arcsin(x) \iff \sin(y) = x, \quad y \in [-\pi/2, \pi/2] $$ $$ y = \arccos(x) \iff \cos(y) = x, \quad y \in [0, \pi] $$ $$ y = \arctan(x) \iff \tan(y) = x, \quad y \in (-\pi/2, \pi/2) $$

2. Robotics & Inverse Kinematics

The `atan2` Advantage

Standard `arctan(y/x)` has a problem: it can't distinguish between Quadrant I and Quadrant III. In AI for Self-Driving Cars or Robot Arms, we use `atan2(y, x)` which takes two arguments to correctly identify the full 360-degree orientation.

  • Inverse Kinematics: "The user wants the robot hand at (x, y). What angle should the motor turn?" This is solved using inverse trig.
  • Object Detection: Predicting the "heading" or orientation of a vehicle in an image requires calculating inverse tangents of predicted bounding box coordinates.

3. Python: atan vs atan2

import math

# Point in Quadrant III: (-1, -1)
x, y = -1, -1

# Wrong way (returns 45° - Quadrant I)
angle_bad = math.degrees(math.atan(y/x)) 

# Right way (returns -135° - Quadrant III)
angle_good = math.degrees(math.atan2(y, x))

print(f"Standard atan: {angle_bad}°")
print(f"Correct atan2: {angle_good}°")
