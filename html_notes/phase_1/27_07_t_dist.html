07. Student's t-Distribution

The Normal distribution's "honest" cousin for small samples.

1. Definition

The Student's t-Distribution arises when we want to estimate the mean of a normally distributed population in situations where the sample size is small ($n < 30$) and the population standard deviation is unknown.

$$ f(t) = \frac{\Gamma(\frac{\nu+1}{2})}{\sqrt{\nu\pi}\Gamma(\frac{\nu}{2})} \left( 1 + \frac{t^2}{\nu} \right)^{-\frac{\nu+1}{2}} $$

Where $\nu$ (nu) represents the Degrees of Freedom ($\nu = n-1$).

2. "Fat Tails"

Compared to a Normal distribution, the t-distribution has "heavier" (fatter) tails. This reflects the greater uncertainty (more spread) that comes from working with fewer data points. As the degrees of freedom increase ($\nu \to \infty$), the t-distribution converges exactly to the Standard Normal Distribution ($Z$).

3. AI/ML Connection: Model Evaluation and Trust

Is our AI better?

The t-distribution is the math behind a **T-Test**, the tool for proving an AI improvement is real.

When you run 10-fold **Cross-Validation**, you get 10 accuracy scores. To determine if Model A is statistically significantly better than Model B, you use a **Paired T-Test** which relies on the t-distribution. It is also used to calculate **Confidence Intervals** for your model's parameters; if an AI predicts a house price with a margin of error, that margin is often calculated using a t-score because we are estimating from a limited sample of training data. Understanding the t-distribution prevents "False Discovery"—claiming your AI is better when the difference was just random noise.

4. Python: Working with t-Distribution

from scipy.stats import t

# Sample size n=10, so df=9
df = 9

# Find the t-score for 95% confidence (two-tailed)
# alpha = 0.05, so we look for 1 - 0.05/2 = 0.975
t_score = t.ppf(0.975, df)
print(f"95% Confidence T-Score: {t_score:.4f}") # ~2.26
# (Contrast with Z-score: 1.96)

# Probability distribution density
print(f"Density at 0: {t.pdf(0, df):.4f}")
