13. Skewness and Kurtosis

Going beyond the mean: analyzing distribution asymmetry and tails.

1. Skewness (The 3rd Standardized Moment)

Skewness measures the asymmetry of a distribution about its mean.

  • Positive Skew (Right-skewed): The "tail" on the right side is longer. Mean > Median.
  • Negative Skew (Left-skewed): The "tail" on the left side is longer. Mean < Median.
$$ \gamma_1 = E\left[ \left( \frac{X - \mu}{\sigma} \right)^3 \right] $$

2. Kurtosis (The 4th Standardized Moment)

Kurtosis measures the "tailedness" of the distribution. It tells you how often extreme values (outliers) occur relative to a normal distribution.

  • Leptokurtic (High Kurtosis): Sharp peak and fat tails (lots of outliers).
  • Platykurtic (Low Kurtosis): Flat peak and thin tails (few outliers).

3. AI/ML Connection: Data Preprocessing and Outliers

Is your model robust?

Analyzing skewness and kurtosis is the first step in **Exploratory Data Analysis (EDA)**.

If a feature has high **Skewness** (e.g., Household Income), Linear Regression will perform poorly because the outliers will "pull" the line away from the majority of data points. We often use Log Transformations to reduce skewness. High **Kurtosis** warns the AI engineer that the dataset contains "heavy tails," meaning the model might encounter extreme values it didn't see during training. Algorithms like **Isolation Forests** (for Anomaly Detection) rely on these higher-order moments to separate "normal" patterns from suspicious ones. If your dataset has high kurtosis, you might switch from **MSE Loss** (sensitive to outliers) to **Huber Loss** (robust to outliers).

4. Python: Shape Analysis

from scipy.stats import skew, kurtosis

# Positive skewed data (e.g., exponential)
data = np.random.exponential(scale=2, size=1000)

print(f"Skewness: {skew(data):.4f}") # Should be > 0
print(f"Kurtosis: {kurtosis(data):.4f}") # (Fisher's definition: Normal = 0)
