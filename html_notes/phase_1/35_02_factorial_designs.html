02. Factorial Designs

Testing everyone at once: The efficiency of interactions.

1. The $2^k$ Design

A Factorial Design allows researchers to study the effect of two or more independent variables (Factors) simultaneously. In a "$2 \times 2$" design, we test two factors, each with two levels (e.g., 'Color' [Red/Blue] and 'Button Size' [Small/Big]).

$$ \text{Total Runs} = \text{Level}_1 \times \text{Level}_2 \times \dots \times \text{Level}_k $$

2. The Interaction Advantage

The biggest benefit isn't speed; it's Interactions. Maybe a 'Big Button' is better on mobile but a 'Small Button' is better on Desktop. A standard "One Factor at a Time" (OFAT) experiment would miss this synergy entirely. Factorial designs reveal how the world works together.

3. AI/ML Connection: Hyperparameter Tuning

Optimizing Global Performance

In AI, Factorial Design is the mathematical heart of Grid Search** and **Orthogonal Array Testing**.

When you tune a **Random Forest**, you don't just tune `max_depth` and then tune `n_estimators`. You tune them together because their effects are coupled. An AI model might fail with 100 trees and depth 2, but succeed with 100 trees and depth 10. This is an Interaction. Furthermore, AutoML Systems use "Fractional Factorial Designs" to explore thousands of hyperparameter combinations in only a few dozen runs, dramatically reducing training costs while finding the "Sweet Spot" of model performance. Understanding these designs allows AI engineers to move from "Guesswork" to "Engineering-grade optimization."

4. Python: Creating a Design Matrix

# 2x2 Factorial combinations
colors = ['Red', 'Blue']
sizes = ['Small', 'Big']

design = pd.DataFrame([(c, s) for c in colors for s in sizes], 
                    columns=['Color', 'Size'])
print(design)
