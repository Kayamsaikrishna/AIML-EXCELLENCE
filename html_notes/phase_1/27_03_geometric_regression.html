Stats 1.7 Linear Algebra

Geometric Interpretation of Regression

Viewing regression not as a formula, but as a projection of truth onto a simpler plane.

The Shadow on the Plane

Imagine your Data is a vector in N-dimensional space.

A Linear Model only allows for a "subset" of that space (a line or a plane). Regression is the process of finding the point in that plane that is closest to the original vector.

1. The Orthogonality Condition

$$ \hat{\mathbf{y}} = \text{proj}_{\text{Col}(\mathbf{X})} \mathbf{y} $$

For the distance to be minimized, the Residual Vector (\(\mathbf{y} - \hat{\mathbf{y}}\)) must be orthogonal (at 90 degrees) to every column in the input matrix \(\mathbf{X}\).

$$ \mathbf{X}^T (\mathbf{y} - \mathbf{X}\hat{\beta}) = 0 $$

2. ML Connection: Manifold Learning

Representation Geometry

Why Projections Matter

Geometric regression is the simplest form of Dimensionality Reduction.

When we project data onto a line, we are saying that only 1 dimension matters. Modern ML takes this further: - PCA: Projects data onto the directions of maximum variance. - Autoencoders: Project high-dimensional data (like images) onto a non-linear "latent" manifold.

Understanding that regression is an "Orthogonal Projection" helps you visualize why high-dimensional features (Multicollinearity) cause issues: when two input vectors are nearly identical, the plane they form is thin and "wobbly," making the projection (the prediction) extremely unstable.
