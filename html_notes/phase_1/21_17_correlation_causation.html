Prob 1.4 The Great Trap

Correlation vs. Causation

Why statistics can be an excellent servant but a dangerous master.

The Common Fallacy

"Cum Hoc Ergo Propter Hoc" (With this, therefore because of this).

Just because two variables move together doesn't mean one drives the other. They could be driven by a Confounding Variable, or it could be pure coincidence (Spurious Correlation).

1. Spurious Correlations

If you search hard enough in a dataset with 1,000 features, you will find some that are 0.99 correlated purely by chance. This is why p-values and Cross-validation are critical—to ensure the relationship generalizes.

2. Simpson's Paradox

A trend appears in several different groups of data but disappears or reverses when these groups are combined.

Example: The Medical Trial

- Hospital A uses Drug X and has a 90% success rate. - Hospital B uses Drug Y and has an 80% success rate.
Combined: Drug Y looks better.
Why? Hospital B takes the "difficult" cases that are harder to treat. When you condition on Severity of Case, Drug X is actually better in both groups. Ignoring the confounder (severity) leads to the wrong conclusion.

3. ML Connection: Causal Inference

Product ML A/B Testing

The $do$-calculus

Standard Machine Learning only cares about $P(Y \mid X)$. Causal Inference cares about $P(Y \mid do(X))$.

If we see that users who use Feature A have higher retention, does Feature A cause retention? Or do power users just naturally use Feature A?
If we force a random group to use Feature A (the $do$-operator), and retention goes up, then we have evidence of causation. This is why **Randomized Controlled Trials (RCTs)** or A/B Tests are the gold standard for product decisions.

4. Causal Graphs (DAGs)

We represent relationships using Directed Acyclic Graphs. - Chain: \(X \to Y \to Z\) (Indirect effect). - Fork: \(Y \gets X \to Z\) (\(X\) is a confounder). - Collider: \(X \to Y \gets Z\) (Conditioning on \(Y\) creates a fake correlation between \(X\) and \(Z\)).
