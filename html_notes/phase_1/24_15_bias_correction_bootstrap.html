Bias Correction via Bootstrap

Using the bootstrap not just for uncertainty, but to actively fix our original guesses.

The Feedback Loop

Sometimes a statistical estimator is fundamentally biased (it systematically over- or under-estimates the truth). The Bootstrap can detect this pattern by comparing your original estimate to the average of your bootstrap estimates.

1. Estimating Bias

$$ \text{Bias}_{boot} = \bar{\hat{\theta}}^* - \hat{\theta} $$
  • \(\hat{\theta}\): Your original estimate from the whole dataset.
  • \(\bar{\hat{\theta}}^*\): The average of your 1,000 bootstrap estimates.

If the bootstrap average is consistently higher than your original guess, it's a sign that your original guess is biased low.

2. The Bias-Corrected Estimate

$$ \hat{\theta}_{corrected} = \hat{\theta} - \text{Bias}_{boot} = 2\hat{\theta} - \bar{\hat{\theta}}^* $$

We subtract the estimated bias from our original guess to get a "cleaner" answer that is closer to the true population value.

3. ML Connection: Small Sample Validation

Supervised Hyperparameters

Correcting Model Performance

When validating a model on a very small dataset (e.g., 50 samples), the measured accuracy is often biased because the test set is so small.

By using Bias-Corrected Bootstrap, we can get a more realistic estimate of how the model will perform on the general population. This prevents "Optimism Bias"—the tendency for models to look better on their own small test sets than they actually are in production.

Parameter Tuning

In complex models like Support Vector Machines (SVM), some internal parameters might be biased based on the sample size. Advanced bootstrap methods are used to correct these parameters during the training phase.
