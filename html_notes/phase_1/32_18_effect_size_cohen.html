18. Effect Size (Cohen's d)

Measuring the MAGNITUDE of change, not just its existence.

1. The Significance Trap

With a large enough sample size ($n=1,000,000$), almost any tiny difference becomes "Statistically Significant" ($p < 0.05$). But is a 0.0001% increase in model accuracy worth a $10M cloud bill? This is where Effect Size comes in. It measures **Magnitude** independent of sample size.

2. Cohen's d

The most common measure for comparing two means. It calculates how many standard deviations separate the two groups.

$$ d = \frac{\bar{x}_1 - \bar{x}_2}{s_{pooled}} $$

Guidelines: $d=0.2$ (Small), $d=0.5$ (Medium), $d=0.8$ (Large).

3. AI/ML Connection: Practical Impact and Model ROI

Is the juice worth the squeeze?

In AI Deployment, Effect Size is the language of Product Management.

If you have a **Recommendation Engine** and your new deep-learning model is "Statistically Significant" over the base model, the next question is: "By how much?". If the **Cohen's d** is 0.1, the gain is "Negligible" even if the p-value is 0.0001. A low effect size means that while the improvement is "Real," it is so small that users won't notice it. In AutoML Search**, we filter results by Effect Size. We only care about hyperparameters that move the metric by a "Medium" or "Large" margin. This ensures that the AI team focuses on high-impact innovations rather than wasting time "polishing sand"—optimizing for tiny gains that have zero impact on the final business goal.

4. Python: Calculating d

# Mean scores and SDs of two models
m1, s1 = 0.82, 0.04
m2, s2 = 0.85, 0.05

# Standard Pooled SD formula
pooled_sd = np.sqrt((s1**2 + s2**2) / 2)
d = (m2 - m1) / pooled_sd

print(f"Cohen's d: {d:.2f}")
# d = 0.67. This is a "Medium to Large" effect. 
# It's definitely worth deploying the new model!
