Prob 1.4 The Square

The Chi-Square Distribution (\(\chi^2\))

Measuring squared deviations from the expected.

Sum of Squared Normals

If you take a standard normal variable \(Z\) and square it, what distribution do you get?
Answer: Chi-Square with 1 Degree of Freedom.

If you sum \(k\) such independent squared normals, you get \(\chi^2\) with \(k\) degrees of freedom.

1. Definition

Let \(Z_1, Z_2, \dots, Z_k\) be independent \(\mathcal{N}(0, 1)\) variables. $$ X = \sum_{i=1}^k Z_i^2 \sim \chi^2(k) $$ The PDF is a special case of the Gamma distribution with \(\alpha = k/2\) and \(\beta = 1/2\).
  • Values are always non-negative (\(x \ge 0\)).
  • Skewed to the right.
  • As \(k\) (df) increases, it looks more like a Normal distribution.

2. Moments

  • Mean: \(E[X] = k\) (The number of degrees of freedom).
  • Variance: \(\text{Var}(X) = 2k\).

3. Statistical Testing (Independence)

Feature Selection Pearson Test

Chi-Square Test for Independence

Suppose you want to know if 'Gender' is related to 'Interest in Tech'. You collect a table of counts.

The Chi-Square statistic measures the distance between your Observed counts and the Expected counts (what you'd see if they were independent). $$ \chi^2 = \sum \frac{(O-E)^2}{E} $$ If the value is very high, you reject the idea that they are independent.

4. Link to Variance

If we have \(n\) samples from a Normal population, the sample variance \(s^2\) is related to the chi-square distribution: $$ \frac{(n-1)s^2}{\sigma^2} \sim \chi^2(n-1) $$ This is how we calculate Confidence Intervals for Variance.
