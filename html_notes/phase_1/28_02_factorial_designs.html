Stats 1.8 Multivariate Exploration

Factorial Designs

Testing everything at once. Why the interaction between variables is often more important than the variables themselves.

The OFAT Error

Most people use One-Factor-At-a-Time (OFAT) experiments. They change one thing, test it, then change another.

The Failure: OFAT misses the interaction. For example: A new drug might work great at 30°C but fail at 40°C. If you only test "Temp" and "Drug" separately, you will never see the disaster coming.

1. The \(2^k\) Design

The most common factorial design is the \(2^k\) design, where you have \(k\) factors, and each factor has 2 levels (e.g., Low and High).

$$ \text{Total Runs} = 2^k $$

If you have 3 factors (Temperature, Pressure, Time):
\( 2^3 = 8 \) different combinations to test.

2. Main Effects vs. Interactions

Main Effect

The average change in the outcome when one factor is moved from its low to high level, averaged over all levels of the other factors.

Interaction Effect

When the effect of Factor A is different depending on the level of Factor B. This is the "Synergy" or "Conflict".

In a Factorial Design, if an interaction is significant, you cannot interpret the Main Effects alone. The interaction "steals" the meaning.

3. ML Connection: Hyperparameter Tuning

AutoML Optimization

Grid Search is a Factorial Design

When you use `GridSearchCV` in scikit-learn, you are performing a Full Factorial Design in the space of your hyperparameters.

  • The Curse of Dimensionality: Factorial designs explode. A 10-hyperparameter grid with 5 levels each requires \(5^{10}\) training runs. This is why we switch to Random Search or Bayesian Optimization for high-dimensional tuning.
  • Feature Crosses: In Deep Learning (e.g., Recommendation Systems), we manually create "Interaction Terms" between Categorical features (User_ID x Ad_ID). This is essentially encoding a factorial relationship directly into the model's architecture.
  • Fractional Factorial: If you have 20 variables, you can't test all \(2^{20}\) combinations. You use "Fractional" designs that test a carefully chosen subset to capture the most important effects (The "Pareto Principle" of variables).
Engineer's Pro-Tip: If your model performs differently on weekends vs. weekdays, you have a Time x Factor Interaction. A simple linear model will miss this unless you use an Interaction Term or a Factorial approach to splitting your data.
