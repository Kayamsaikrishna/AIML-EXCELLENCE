06. Mean Squared Error (MSE)

The ultimate measure of estimator quality.

1. Definition

The Mean Squared Error (MSE) of an estimator measures the average of the squares of the "errors"—that is, the average squared difference between the estimator and the parameter being estimated.

$$ MSE(\hat{\theta}) = E[(\hat{\theta} - \theta)^2] $$

2. The Bias-Variance Decomposition

One of the most important proofs in statistics shows that MSE is composed of two independent terms:

$$ MSE(\hat{\theta}) = Var(\hat{\theta}) + [Bias(\hat{\theta})]^2 $$

This means that to have a good estimator (low MSE), you must minimize both the **Systematic Error** (Bias) and the **Randomness** (Variance). Often, efforts to reduce one will increase the other.

3. AI/ML Connection: The Loss Function Objective

The Target of Learning

In AI, MSE is the most common **Loss Function** for Regression.

When you train an AI using `MeanSquaredError` as the criterion, you are explicitly asking the model to find parameters that balance the Bias-Variance Tradeoff. If you use a very complex model (like a 100-layer ResNet), your Bias will be low (low training error), but your Variance will be very high (Overfitting—high MSE on new data). If you use a simple model, your Variance will be low, but your Bias will be high (Underfitting). The goal of Regularization** and **Cross-Validation** is to find the "Sweet Spot" in the MSE curve where the sum of squared bias and variance is at its absolute minimum.

4. Python: Calculating decomposition

# Imagine we repeat an estimation 1000 times
estimates = np.array([10.5, 11.2, 9.8, 10.1, ...])
true_theta = 10.0

# Components
bias = np.mean(estimates) - true_theta
variance = np.var(estimates)

# Formulas
mse_direct = np.mean((estimates - true_theta)**2)
mse_decomposed = variance + bias**2

print(f"MSE: {mse_direct:.4f}")
print(f"Bias^2 + Var: {mse_decomposed:.4f}") # They are mathematically identical!
