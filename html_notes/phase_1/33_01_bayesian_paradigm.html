01. Bayesian vs. Frequentist Paradigms

Two different ways of viewing the truth.

1. The Core Conflict

In Statistics, there are two major schools of thought regarding probability and parameters:

  • The Frequentist View: Probability is the long-run frequency of events. Parameters (like model weights) are **Fixed but Unknown**. Data is random. We ask: "How likely is this data, given a fixed truth?".
  • The Bayesian View: Probability is a **Degree of Belief**. Parameters are **Random Variables** themselves. We have a belief about them before seeing data, and we update that belief after seeing data. We ask: "What is the probability of the truth, given this data?".

2. The Comparison

Feature Frequentist Bayesian
Parameters Fixed points Probability distributions
Prior Knowledge Ignored (only data matters) Essential (incorporated as "Priors")
Result p-values/Confidence Intervals Posterior distributions/Credible Intervals

3. AI/ML Connection: Model Uncertainty and Active Learning

Knowing what you don't know

In AI, the Bayesian paradigm is the foundation of Epistemic Uncertainty.

Standard Neural Networks are Frequentist: they output a single set of weights. But what if the model is guessing? A **Bayesian Neural Network** doesn't have one set of weights; it has a distribution for every weight. If the weights for a specific prediction are "Wide" (High variance), the AI can say: "I'm predicting 'Cat', but I am very uncertain." This is critical for Active Learning: the AI can identify data points it is most confused about and ask a human to label them, rather than wasting labeling budget on things it already "Knows." Bayesian methods allow AI to be **Self-Aware** about its own ignorance—a vital step toward safe and reliable artificial intelligence.

4. Python: The Two Perspectives

# Scenario: Estimating the fairness of a coin (True p=0.7)
data = np.random.binomial(1, 0.7, 10)

# Frequentist: Point Estimate (MLE)
mle = np.mean(data)
print(f"Frequentist Guess: P(Head) = {mle}")

# Bayesian: Update a Prior (Thinking it's fair: Beta(2,2))
from scipy.stats import beta
heads = sum(data)
tails = len(data) - heads
# Posterior of Beta(2+heads, 2+tails)
x = np.linspace(0, 1, 100)
posterior = beta.pdf(x, 2 + heads, 2 + tails)
# The Bayesian result is a WHOLE CURVE, not just one number.
