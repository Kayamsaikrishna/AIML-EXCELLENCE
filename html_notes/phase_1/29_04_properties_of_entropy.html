Info Theory Math Laws

Properties of Entropy

Universal Truths of Uncertainty. The mathematical axioms that define how "Information" behaves across systems.

The Concave Nature of Knowledge

Entropy is fundamentally concave. This means that mixing two different sources of information always increases (or keeps constant) the total uncertainty.

"A little bit of two different rumors is more confusing than a lot of just one."

1. Core Mathematical Properties

1. Concavity

For any two distributions \(p\) and \(q\) and \(\lambda \in [0, 1]\):

$$ H(\lambda p + (1-\lambda)q) \ge \lambda H(p) + (1-\lambda) H(q) $$

This ensures that optimization problems involving entropy are generally stable and well-behaved.

2. Non-negativity & Bounds

For a discrete random variable \(X\) with \(n\) outcomes:

$$ 0 \le H(X) \le \log_2 n $$

\(H=0\) means certainty. \(H=\log n\) means uniform ignorance.

3. Grouping Rule

Entropy is additive across splits. If you group outcomes into subgroups, the total entropy is the entropy of the subgroups plus the weighted entropy within each subgroup.

2. Interaction Laws

Independence Additivity

If \(X\) and \(Y\) are independent random variables:

$$ H(X, Y) = H(X) + H(Y) $$

Knowing one tells you absolutely nothing about the other.

Conditioning Reduces Entropy

A fundamental theorem of Information Theory:

$$ H(X|Y) \le H(X) $$

"Information can never hurt." Knowing \(Y\) will either help you predict \(X\) or change nothing, but it will never make you more uncertain.

Optimization ELBO

The Logic of the ELBO

In Variational Inference, we want to find a simple distribution \(q\) that approximates a complex one \(p\). We use Jensen's Inequality, which relies on the concave property of the Logarithm (and thus Entropy):

$$ \log E[X] \ge E[\log X] $$

This allows us to construct the Evidence Lower Bound (ELBO), which is the foundational loss function for Variational Autoencoders (VAEs). We optimize a "lower bound" because calculating the direct log-likelihood is often mathematically impossible.

Why Stability Matters: Because entropy is concave, gradient descent won't get "confused" by local spikes in uncertainty during training—it will always try to move toward the smoothed maximum or minimum.
