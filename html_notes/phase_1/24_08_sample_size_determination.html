Sample Size Determination

Answering the executive question: "How much data do we actually need to be sure?"

The Cost of Certainty

In Machine Learning, data is expensive. Labeling costs time and money. In A/B testing, running a test too long wastes traffic, but running it too short leads to incorrect conclusions (False Positives).

Sample Size Determination calculates the minimum $n$ required to achieve a desired Margin of Error.

1. The Formula for Mean

To achieve a Margin of Error \(E\): $$ n = \left( \frac{z^* \cdot \sigma}{E} \right)^2 $$
  • \(z^*\): Desired confidence (e.g., 1.96 for 95%).
  • \(\sigma\): Estimated population standard deviation (from pilot study).
  • \(E\): Maximum allowable error (the "precision" you want).

2. The Formula for Proportion

$$ n = \hat{p}(1-\hat{p}) \left( \frac{z^*}{E} \right)^2 $$

Pro Tip: If you have no idea what \(\hat{p}\) is, use 0.5. This gives the largest possible sample size (The "Worst Case" scenario), ensuring your study is safe.

3. ML Connection: Budgeting for Labels

Project Management Data Annotation

How many images should I label?

If you are building a medical diagnosis AI and you want your measured accuracy to be accurate within ±2% (at 95% confidence):

  1. Assume \(\hat{p} = 0.5\) (Conservative).
  2. Target Error \(E = 0.02\).
  3. \(n = 0.5(1-0.5) \times (1.96 / 0.02)^2 \approx \mathbf{2,401}\).

This tells you exactly how many images you need to send to your labeling team. Labeling 10,000 would be a waste of money; labeling 500 would make your accuracy numbers unreliable.

A/B Power Analysis

In industry (e.g., Netflix, Amazon), this is called Power Analysis. It prevents the "Peeking Problem"—checking the results every day and stopping when the chart looks good. By setting the sample size upfront, you ensure the result is statistically valid.
