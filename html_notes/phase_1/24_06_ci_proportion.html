Stats 1.4 Binary Outcomes

CI for Proportions

Measuring the uncertainty in "Yes/No" questions, like model accuracy or conversion rates.

The Accuracy Range

Success or Failure (0 or 1) follows a Binomial distribution. When we have a reasonably large sample (\(n\hat{p} \ge 10\) and \(n(1-\hat{p}) \ge 10\)), we can approximate this with a Normal distribution to build a Confidence Interval.

1. The Formula (Wald Interval)

$$ \hat{p} \pm z_{\alpha/2} \sqrt{\frac{\hat{p}(1-\hat{p})}{n}} $$
  • \(\hat{p}\): Sample Proportion (e.g., \(80/100 = 0.8\)).
  • \(\sqrt{\frac{\hat{p}(1-\hat{p})}{n}}\): The Standard Error of a Proportion.

2. Margin of Error (MoE)

The term after the \(\pm\) is the Margin of Error. - To cut the MoE in half, you need to quadruple the sample size (\(n\)). - This is the diminishing returns of data collection.

3. ML Connection: Accuracy Reporting

Classification Product Readiness

Is 90% Accuracy enough?

If you test your model on 100 images and get 90% accuracy: - Your 95% CI is roughly [84%, 96%].
If you test on 1,000 images and get 90%: - Your 95% CI is roughly [88%, 92%].

Why this matters: If a safety critical system requires 88% minimum accuracy to be legal, the first model is "unreliable" because the truth could be 84% (lower than the limit). The second model is "verified" because the entire confidence interval is above the 88% threshold.
