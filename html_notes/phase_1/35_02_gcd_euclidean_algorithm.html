Number Theory Algorithms

GCD & The Euclidean Algorithm

Finding Common Ground. Mastery of the mathematical process for identifying the largest shared factor between two integers—the basis of modular inverses.

Common Factors

The Greatest Common Divisor (GCD) of two integers $a$ and $b$ is the largest integer $d$ such that $d|a$ and $d|b$. If $gcd(a, b) = 1$, we say $a$ and $b$ are relatively prime or coprime.

1. The Euclidean Algorithm

Based on the observation that $gcd(a, b) = gcd(b, a \pmod b)$. We repeat the division algorithm until the remainder is zero.

$$ a = bq_1 + r_1 $$ $$ b = r_1q_2 + r_2 $$ $$ r_1 = r_2q_3 + r_3 $$ $$ \dots $$ $$ r_{n-1} = r_nq_{n+1} + 0 \implies \text{GCD is } r_n $$

This algorithm is incredibly efficient, with a time complexity of $O(\log(\min(a, b)))$.

2. Bézout's Identity

For any integers $a$ and $b$, there exist integers $x$ and $y$ such that:

$$ ax + by = gcd(a, b) $$

The Extended Euclidean Algorithm provides a way to calculate these coefficients $x$ and $y$. In cryptography, this is how we find the modular multiplicative inverse.

Cryptography Hashing

Why AI researchers use GCD

  • Modular Inverses: Calculating $1/a \pmod n$ is required for many secure multi-party computation (SMPC) protocols used in privacy-preserving AI. This only exists if $gcd(a, n) = 1$.
  • Coprime Sizing: When designing hash tables or random sequences, choosing coprime parameters ensures that the entire space is "covered" before repeating. This helps avoid cycles in data sampling.
  • RSA Key Gen: Generating public and private keys requires selecting an exponent $e$ such that $gcd(e, \phi(n)) = 1$.
  • Image Resizing: Finding the GCD of image dimensions helps determine the "Aspect Ratio" or the largest possible grid size for tile-based processing.
