05. The Complement Rule

The efficiency of working with what didn't happen.

1. Defining the Complement

The Complement of an event \(A\), denoted as \(A^c\) (or \(\bar{A}\)), is the set of all outcomes in the sample space \(S\) that are not in \(A\).

$$ P(A) + P(A^c) = 1 $$ $$ P(A) = 1 - P(A^c) $$

2. Why is this useful?

Sometimes, calculating the probability that an event *doesn't* happen is much easier than calculating all the ways it *could* happen.

Example: "What is the probability of getting at least one 6 in three rolls of a die?" To solve this directly, you'd calculate for exactly 1, exactly 2, and exactly 3. Using the complement rule, you just find \(1 - P(\text{no sixes at all})\).

3. AI/ML Connection: Negative Sampling

Mining the Silence

In Recommendation Engines (like YouTube or Netflix) and Word Embeddings (Word2Vec), we have billions of possible items. Calculating the probability of what a user likes out of everything is slow.

Instead, we use Negative Sampling. We treat the items a user didn't click on as the "Complement" of their interests. By optimizing for \(1 - P(\text{Negative Sample})\), we can train models thousands of times faster. Basically, the model learns what you like by also learning exactly what you don't like. This "Symmetry" of probability is what makes large-scale AI computationally feasible.

4. Python: The "At Least One" Trick

# Experiment: Probability of rolling at least one 6 in 5 rolls
p_no_six = (5/6)**5
p_at_least_one = 1 - p_no_six

print(f"P(No Six): {p_no_six:.4f}")
print(f"P(At least one Six): {p_at_least_one:.4f}")
