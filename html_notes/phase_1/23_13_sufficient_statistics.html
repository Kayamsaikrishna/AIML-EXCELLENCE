Stats 1.4 Compression

Sufficient Statistics

Reducing a massive database into a few numbers without losing ANY information about the reality you're predicting.

The Efficient Memory

A statistic \(T(X)\) is Sufficient for a parameter \(\theta\) if, given the value of \(T(X)\), the original data \(X\) provides no additional information about \(\theta\).

"If I tell you the sum of 1,000 coin flips is 500, knowing the exact order of the heads and tails tells you nothing more about how biased the coin is."

1. Fisher-Neyman Factorization Theorem

A statistic \(T(X)\) is sufficient if and only if the likelihood can be split: $$ L(X \mid \theta) = g(T(X), \theta) \cdot h(X) $$

Here, \(h(X)\) depends only on the data (not \(\theta\)), and \(g\) depends only on the summary \(T(X)\) and the parameter.

2. Minimal Sufficiency

A Minimal Sufficient Statistic is the most compressed version of the data possible that still captures everything about \(\theta\). - For a Normal distribution with unknown \(\mu\), the sample mean \(\bar{x}\) is the minimal sufficient statistic. You don't need the individual 1,000 data points; you just need their average.

3. ML Connection: Feature Engineering

Foundational Deep Learning

Sufficient Representations

The goal of the early layers of a Neural Network is to find a Sufficient Representation of the input.

If we are predicting "Cat vs Dog", the raw pixels (1 million numbers) contain the information. A good model compresses those pixels into a "Feature Vector" (1,000 numbers). If that feature vector is a "Sufficient Statistic", the final classification layer doesn't need to look at the original pixels at all—everything it needs to know is in the compressed vector.
