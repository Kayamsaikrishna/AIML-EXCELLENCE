Limit Comparison Test (LCT)

Seeing the long-term behavior.

Ignoring the Noise

Sometimes direct comparison is hard because of messy terms like \(+1\) or \(-5\). LCT says: "As \(n \to \infty\), does the ratio of the two series settle to a constant?"

\[ L = \lim_{n \to \infty} \frac{a_n}{b_n} \]

If \(L\) is positive and finite (\(0 < L < \infty\)), then \(a_n\) and \(b_n\) behave **identically**. They either both converge or both diverge.

2. AI/ML Connection: Big-O Complexity

Asymptotic Growth

This is the exact mathematical foundation of Big-O Notation in Computer Science. When we say an algorithm is \(O(n^2)\), we mean that: \[ \lim_{n \to \infty} \frac{\text{Runtime}(n)}{n^2} = C \]

We ignore simpler terms and coefficients because for massive datasets (large \(n\)), only the dominant term matters. Just like in LCT, we strip away the "noise" to classify the algorithm's scalability class.

3. Python: Calculating the Limit Ratio

lct_check.py
# Series A: (n + 5) / (2n^3 - 1)
# Dominant terms: n / 2n^3 = 1 / 2n^2
# Compare with Series B: 1 / n^2

def get_ratio(n):
    a_n = (n + 5) / (2*n**3 - 1)
    b_n = 1 / (n**2)
    return a_n / b_n

print(f"{'n':<10} | {'Ratio a_n/b_n':<15}")
print("-" * 30)

for n in [10, 100, 1000, 10000]:
    print(f"{n:<10} | {get_ratio(n):<15.6f}")

# Ratio approaches 0.5.
# Since ratio is finite constant, and 1/n^2 converges, A converges.
