1. Construction

Every linear transformation \( T: \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}^m \) can be represented by a unique \( m \times n \) matrix \( A \).

To find \( A \), simply apply \( T \) to the standard basis vectors \( e_1, \dots, e_n \). The results form the columns of \( A \).

$$ A = \begin{bmatrix} | & & | \\ T(e_1) & \dots & T(e_n) \\ | & & | \end{bmatrix} $$

2. Convolution as Matrix Mult

Even complex operations like Image Convolution are linear transformations. A \( 3 \times 3 \) convolution on a \( 10 \times 10 \) image can be represented as a massive sparse matrix multiplication (Toeplitz Matrix) acting on the flattened image vector.

This is why GPUs (matrix multipliers) are so good at training CNNs.

3. Building the Matrix

import numpy as np

# Define a transformation T: Shear
# x -> x + y
# y -> y
def T_shear(v):
    x, y = v
    return np.array([x + y, y])

# Basis Vectors
e1 = np.array([1, 0])
e2 = np.array([0, 1])

# Apply T to basis
Te1 = T_shear(e1) # Becomes [1, 0]
Te2 = T_shear(e2) # Becomes [1, 1]

# Construct Matrix A
A = np.column_stack((Te1, Te2))
print("Transformation Matrix A:\n", A)

# Verify
v = np.array([2, 3])
Tv_func = T_shear(v)
Tv_mat = A @ v
print(f"Function Result: {Tv_func}")
print(f"Matrix Result:   {Tv_mat}")
# Match confirmed
