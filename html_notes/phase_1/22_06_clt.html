Prob 1.4 The King of Stats

Central Limit Theorem (CLT)

Why almost everything becomes a Bell Curve if you sum enough of it.

The Universal Attractor

The Laws of Large Numbers tell us where the average goes (the mean). The **Central Limit Theorem** tells us the shape of the fluctuations around that mean.

"Sum a million dice rolls, or a million coin flips, or a million heights—the result is always a Normal Distribution."

1. The Formal Statement

Let \(X_1, X_2, \dots, X_n\) be i.i.d. with mean \(\mu\) and finite variance \(\sigma^2\). As \(n \to \infty\), the sample mean \(\bar{X}_n\) converges in distribution to: $$ \bar{X}_n \xrightarrow{d} \mathcal{N}\left(\mu, \frac{\sigma^2}{n}\right) $$ Standardized form: $$ Z_n = \frac{\bar{X}_n - \mu}{\sigma/\sqrt{n}} \xrightarrow{d} \mathcal{N}(0, 1) $$

2. Key Requirements

  • Independent: The variables shouldn't influence each other.
  • Finite Variance: This is why the Cauchy Distribution breaks the CLT—it has infinite variance, so its sums never become Normal.
  • Identically Distributed: (Though there are "Generalized" CLTs that relax this).

3. ML Connection: Error Analysis & Confidence

Statistical ML Hypothesis Testing

Confidence Intervals

When we evaluate a model on a test set of size \(n\), the average accuracy we get is a "sample mean". The CLT allows us to say: **"The error of my estimate is Normally distributed around the true accuracy."**

This is how we calculate 95% Confidence Intervals. If we know the variance, we know that the "True" accuracy is within \(\pm 1.96 \sigma/\sqrt{n}\) of our measured accuracy.

Parameter Estimation

In Neural Networks, the sum of many weights and inputs at a single neuron often follows a Normal distribution due to the CLT. This is why Gaussian Initialization works so well—it prepares the network layers for the distribution they will naturally gravitate towards.

4. The Power of Square Root $n$

Notice the \(\sqrt{n}\) in the denominator. This is the Rate of Convergence.

  • If you want to make your error 10 times smaller, you need 100 times more data.
  • This "law of diminishing returns" is why collecting massive datasets is often necessary for high-precision scientific ML.
