02. Prior Distributions

Quantifying what you know before the data arrives.

1. The Starting Point

A Prior Distribution $P(\theta)$ represents your knowledge, experience, or initial assumptions about a parameter before observing any data. There are three main types:

  • Informative Priors: Based on strong previous evidence or physical constraints (e.g., "Weights must be near zero").
  • Non-informative (Flat) Priors: We have no clue, so we assign equal probability to everything (Maximum Entropy).
  • Conjugate Priors: Mathematically "Friendly" priors that result in a posterior of the same family.

2. Subjectivity vs. Objectivity

Critics often say priors are "Subjective." Bayesians argue that **Choosing not to have a prior** is just as subjective—it's equivalent to assuming all values are equally likely, which is rarely true in the real world.

3. AI/ML Connection: Regularization and Pre-training

Priors as Constraints

In AI, priors are everywhere—mostly disguised as Regularization.

L2 Regularization (Weight Decay) is mathematically identical to having a Gaussian Prior** centered at zero for your weights. It says: "Before seeing data, I believe the weights should be small." **L1 Regularization (Lasso)** is equivalent to a **Laplace Prior**, which forces sparsity. Even more powerfully, Transfer Learning is a Bayesian update: the "Base Model" trained on ImageNet acts as a highly Informative Prior** for your specific task. You aren't starting from scratch; you are updating years of "knowledge" about edges and shapes with your small specific dataset. This allows AI to learn from 10 examples what would otherwise take 10,000.

4. Python: Visualizing Priors

from scipy.stats import norm

# Weak Prior: N(0, 10) - We don't know much
x = np.linspace(-30, 30, 100)
weak_p = norm.pdf(x, 0, 10)

# Strong Informative Prior: N(5, 1) - We are quite sure it's 5
strong_p = norm.pdf(x, 5, 1)

plt.plot(x, weak_p, label='Weak')
plt.plot(x, strong_p, label='Strong')
# A strong prior requires MUCH more data to change its 'mind'.
