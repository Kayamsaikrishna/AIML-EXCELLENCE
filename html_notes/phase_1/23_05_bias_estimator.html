Stats 1.4 Systematic Error

Bias of an Estimator

Measuring if your estimate is "hitting the mark" on average.

The Average Mistake

An estimator is Unbiased if, when you repeat the experiment infinitely many times, the average of all your estimates is exactly equal to the true population parameter.

1. The Formula

$$ \text{Bias}(\hat{\theta}) = E[\hat{\theta}] - \theta $$
  • If \(\text{Bias} = 0\), the estimator is Unbiased.
  • If \(\text{Bias} > 0\), it consistently overestimates.
  • If \(\text{Bias} < 0\), it consistently underestimates.

2. The Famous Case: Sample Variance

Why do we divide by \(n-1\) instead of \(n\) for sample variance? - If you divide by \(n\), the estimator is Biased (it consistently underestimates the true variance). - Using \(n-1\) makes the estimator Unbiased. This is known as Bessel's Correction.

3. ML Connection: Underfitting

Foundational Model Complexity

High Bias = Missing the Pattern

In Machine Learning, Bias refers to the error introduced by approximating a real-world problem (which may be complex) by a much simpler model (e.g., trying to fit a line to a curve).

A model with high bias Underfits. It ignores the nuances of the data because its "average" prediction is fundamentally incapable of capturing the true relationship, no matter how much data you give it.
