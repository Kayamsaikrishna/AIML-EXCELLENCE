Stats 1.6 Paradigm Shift

The Bayesian Paradigm

Probability isn't just counting frequencies; it's a measure of our personal "Strength of Belief."

The Fundamental Question

Frequentist: "How many times did this happen in 1,000 trials?"
Bayesian: "Given what I already know, how much do I trust this outcome?"

1. Frequentist vs. Bayesian

Frequentists

  • Parameters are Fixed (but unknown).
  • Data is Random.
  • Goal: Estimate the "One True Value."
  • Weakness: Cannot handle events that only happen once (e.g., "Will it rain tomorrow?").

Bayesians

  • Parameters are Random Variables.
  • Data is Fixed (once observed).
  • Goal: Find the *Distribution* of the truth.
  • Strength: Incorporates "Prior Knowledge" and handles unique events naturally.

2. The "Scientific Method" in a Formula

$$ \text{Posterior} \propto \text{Likelihood} \times \text{Prior} $$

Bayesian thinking is about Updating. You start with an opinion (Prior), you see some data (Likelihood), and you end up with a refined opinion (Posterior).

3. ML Connection: The Cold-Start Problem

Recommendation Personalization

Why Netflix needs a Prior

Imagine a brand-new user joins Netflix. We have zero data on them (Frequentist nightmare: Probability of click = 0/0).

A Bayesian Recommender doesn't start from zero. It uses a Prior Distribution based on "Average Users" in that country. - Prior: Most new users like 'Stranger Things'. - Action: Show them 'Stranger Things'. - Update: If they skip it, the Likelihood of them being "Average" drops, and the Posterior shifts to a specific niche (e.g., Anime lover).

This "Updating as you go" is exactly how Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs) and **Kalman Filters** operate—maintaining a state (belief) that updates with every new timestep.
