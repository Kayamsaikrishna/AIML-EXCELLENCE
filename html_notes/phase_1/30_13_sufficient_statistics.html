13. Sufficient Statistics

Extracting the core information without the bulk.

1. Definition

A statistic $T(X)$ is Sufficient for a parameter $\theta$ if the conditional distribution of the data $X$, given the value of $T(X)$, does not depend on $\theta$.

$$ f(x \mid T(x) = t, \theta) = f(x \mid T(x) = t) $$

This means that $T(X)$ captures **all** the information in the data set that is useful for estimating $\theta$. Once you know the sufficient statistic, you can throw away the raw data without losing any accuracy in your estimate.

2. The Factorization Theorem

We can identify a sufficient statistic using the Neyman-Fisher Factorization Theorem. A statistic $T(X)$ is sufficient if the joint density can be split into two parts: one that only depends on $T(X)$ and $\theta$, and another that only depends on the raw data $X$.

$$ f(x \mid \theta) = g(T(x), \theta) \times h(x) $$

3. AI/ML Connection: Data Reduction and Feature Extraction

The Mathematics of Summarization

In AI, Sufficient Statistics are the theoretical basis for Efficient Data Processing.

In **Online Learning** and **Streaming AI**, we cannot store the entire history of millions of users. Instead, we only store the Sufficient Statistics (e.g., the sum and the sum of squares of user interactions). This allows the AI to update its model as if it had seen the entire history, but with a tiny memory footprint. In **Autoencoders**, the Latent Representation** is essentially a search for a "Sufficient-ish" statistic—a compressed version of the image that contains all the information needed to reconstruct it. Understanding sufficiency helps AI engineers distinguish between "Noise" (data that doesn't help estimate $\theta$) and "Information," leading to much faster training and smaller model files.

4. Python: Sufficiency of the Mean

# If we have two samples with the same mean, they provide identical info about mu.

data2 = np.random.normal(50, 5, 100) # Different raw data
