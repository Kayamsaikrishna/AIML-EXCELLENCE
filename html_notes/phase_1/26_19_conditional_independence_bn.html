Stats 1.6 Inference Logic

Conditional Independence (D-Separation)

Learning when info can travel through a graph and when it is "blocked."

The Traffic Light Analogy

D-Separation is the set of rules that tells us: "If I know variable X, does that help me predict Z?"

1. The Three Basic Atoms

1. The Chain (\(X \rightarrow Y \rightarrow Z\))

Information flows from X to Z. But if you observe Y**, the chain is **Blocked. Z is independent of X given Y.

Example: Smoke \(\rightarrow\) Alarm \(\rightarrow\) Evacuation. If you already hear the Alarm, seeing the Smoke doesn't give you "extra" info about Evacuation.

2. The Fork (\(X \leftarrow Y \rightarrow Z\))

X and Z look correlated (common cause). If you observe Y**, the fork is **Blocked. X and Z are now independent.

Example: Ice Cream Sales \(\leftarrow\) Heatwave \(\rightarrow\) Drownings. Once you know it's a Heatwave, Ice Cream sales don't help predict Drownings.

3. The Collider (\(X \rightarrow Y \leftarrow Z\))

The Weird One! X and Z are already independent. But if you observe Y, they become Correlated!

Example: Talent \(\rightarrow\) Celebrity \(\leftarrow\) Beauty. They are independent. But if you look at "Celebrity" group (Observed Y), and see someone is not Beautiful, you find it's very likely they are highly Talented. This is called Explaining Away.

2. ML Connection: Berkson's Paradox

Data Bias Causality

Why your model finds fake correlations

Imagine a recruitment model that looks only at Top Tech Companies. - Variables: Coding Skill (\(X\)) and Communication Skill (\(Z\)). - Collider: Hired at Google (\(Y\)).

In the general population, coding and talking are independent. But in the "Google Employees" dataset (Observed Collider), they will appear Negatively Correlated. Why? Because to get hired, you need at least ONE of them. If a hire has low coding skills, they MUST have high communication skills to have made the cut.

If your ML model learns from this biased "Success-only" data without understanding D-separation, it will mistakenly conclude that "Good coders are bad at talking." This is a classic Selection Bias error.
