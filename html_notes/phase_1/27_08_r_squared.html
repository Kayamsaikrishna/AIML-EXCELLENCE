Stats 1.7 Goodness of Fit

R-Squared (\(R^2\))

Measuring what percentage of the variation in the target is "explained" by the features.

The Baseline Comparison

\(R^2\) compares your model to the World's Dumbest Model: The Mean (\(\bar{y}\)).

- \(R^2 = 1\): Perfect prediction. - \(R^2 = 0\): Your model is no better than just guessing the average every time. - **\(R^2 < 0\):** Your model is actually *worse* than guessing the average!

1. The Formula

$$ R^2 = 1 - \frac{RSS}{TSS} = 1 - \frac{\sum (y_i - \hat{y}_i)^2}{\sum (y_i - \bar{y})^2} $$

Where: - RSS: Residual Sum of Squares (What we missed). - TSS: Total Sum of Squares (How much the data varies naturally).

2. Interpretability Warnings

High \(R^2\) is NOT the goal

In social sciences, an \(R^2\) of 0.2 is great. In physics, an \(R^2\) of 0.99 is expected. Correlation is not causation!

The Complexity Trap

\(R^2\) never decreases when you add more features. You can add "Number of cats in Brazil" to a model predicting New York stock prices, and \(R^2\) will stay the same or go up. It's a "Greedy" metric.

3. ML Connection: Explained Variance

Regression Dashboards

Why we don't use R-Squared for evaluation

In modern ML (Deep Learning), we rarely look at \(R^2\). We look at MAE or RMSE.

Why? Because \(R^2\) is unit-less. It doesn't tell a developer how far off the prediction is in "Dollars" or "Seconds." It's great for academic papers and general summaries, but for the engineering floor, absolute error (\(y - \hat{y}\)) is king.

However, \(R^2\) IS useful for Feature Selection. If adding a new 100MB embedding vector only moves \(R^2\) by 0.0001, we might decide to discard that feature and save memory.
