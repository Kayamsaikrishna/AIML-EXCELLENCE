Calculus 1.6 Radial

Polar Integration

Don't forget the 'r'!

Circular Symmetry

When integrating simple shapes like circles or Gaussian bells, Cartesian (\( x, y \)) coordinates are painful. Polar Coordinates (\( r, \theta \)) make the bounds constant (e.g., \( r \) from 0 to 1).

The "Transfer Tax" (Jacobian) for this switch is \( r \). \[ \iint_{R} f(x, y) dA = \int_{\theta_1}^{\theta_2} \int_{r_1}^{r_2} f(r \cos \theta, r \sin \theta) \, \mathbf{r} \, dr \, d\theta \] Why \( r \)? Because as you move further out, the arc length "fans out". A small change \( d\theta \) covers more area at \( r=10 \) than at \( r=1 \).

2. AI/ML Connection: The Gaussian Kernel

Why Normal Distributions Integrate to 1

The famous Gaussian Integral \( \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} e^{-x^2} dx = \sqrt{\pi} \) cannot be solved using standard Calculus 1 methods. It is solved by squaring it to create a 2D surface \( e^{-(x^2+y^2)} \) and switching to **Polar Coordinates**.

\[ \int_{0}^{2\pi} \int_{0}^{\infty} e^{-r^2} \mathbf{r} \, dr \, d\theta \] The extra \( r \) allows a simple \( u \)-substitution (\( u=r^2 \)), making the integral solvable. This single math trick is the foundation of Probability Theory, **Standardization (Z-score), and **Radial Basis Function (RBF) Kernels in SVMs.
