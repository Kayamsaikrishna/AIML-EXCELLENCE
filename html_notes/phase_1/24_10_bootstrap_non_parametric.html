Stats 1.4 Data-Driven

Non-parametric Bootstrap

Measuring uncertainty when you have no idea what the underlying distribution is.

The Plug-in Principle

The Non-parametric Bootstrap assumes that the Empirical Distribution (your raw data) is our best possible estimate of the population distribution. We "plug in" our data to act as the world.

1. Why "Non-parametric"?

Classic statistics (Z/T tests) assumes the data is Normal (parameters \(\mu, \sigma\)). Non-parametric Bootstrap doesn't care. Your data could be bimodal, skewed, or have multiple outliers. The bootstrap handles it automatically because it doesn't try to fit the data to a curve—it just re-uses the data points as they are.

2. Handling Complex Metrics

For the sample mean, we have the CLT. But what about: - The Median? - The Correlation Coefficient? - The F1-Score of a model?

There is no simple "F1-Score Table" in the back of a textbook. To find the confidence interval for an F1-score, we MUST use the Bootstrap.

3. ML Connection: Model Robustness Analysis

Deep Learning Evaluation

The Distribution of the AUC

In sensitive fields like Medical Imaging (Detecting Cancer), reporting a single AUC of 0.95 is not enough. Regulatory bodies want to see the 95% Confidence Interval.

The Workflow: 1. Start with test set \(X_{test}\). 2. Create 1,000 bootstrap test sets by resampling \(X_{test}\) with replacement. 3. Calculate AUC for each set. 4. The 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of those 1,000 AUCs give the 95% Confidence Interval.

This "Non-parametric" approach is the standard in research papers because it works even if the model's errors are weirdly distributed.
