01. Randomization in Experimental Design

The only way to prove causality, not just correlation.

1. Beyond Observation

In observation, we just watch what happens. In Experimental Design, we intervene. Randomization is the process of randomly assigning subjects to different groups (Treatment vs. Control) to ensure that any difference in the outcome is caused by the treatment, not by some pre-existing difference between the groups.

2. The Gold Standard

Randomization "Averages out" hidden variables (Confounders). If you have 1,000 people and randomly split them, the average age, income, and health in both groups will be nearly identical. This allows us to use the **Counterfactual** logic: "What would have happened if we didn't do X?"

3. AI/ML Connection: A/B Testing and Causal Discovery

Proving the Model's Worth

In AI, randomization is the core of A/B Testing**.

When you deploy a new **Recommendation Engine**, you don't just launch it for everyone. You randomly assign 5% of users to the new model (Treatment) and keep 95% on the old one (Control). The randomization ensures that if the new model increases revenue, it's because the model is better, not because "Power Users" happened to see it. It is also the foundation of Causal Machine Learning (Double ML)**: we use ML to predict the treatment effect while using randomization logic to ensure the estimated effect is unbiased. Without randomization, AI engineers risk the "Selection Bias" trap, where they think their model is working simply because it's being shown to users who were going to buy the product anyway.

4. Python: Simple Random Assignment

# Assigning 1000 users to A or B
users = np.arange(1000)
np.random.shuffle(users)

group_a = users[:500]  # Control
group_b = users[500:]  # Treatment

print(f"Group A Mean ID: {np.mean(group_a)}")
print(f"Group B Mean ID: {np.mean(group_b)}")
# Should be very similar if randomization is high quality!
