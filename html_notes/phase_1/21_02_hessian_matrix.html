02. The Hessian Matrix

Capturing the curvature of the landscape.

1. Beyond the Gradient

The gradient tells you the slope, but the Hessian Matrix tells you the shape. It is a square matrix composed of every possible second-order partial derivative of a function \(f(x_1, x_2, \dots, x_n)\).

$$ \mathbf{H} = \begin{bmatrix} \frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x_1^2} & \frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x_1 \partial x_2} \\ \frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x_2 \partial x_1} & \frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x_2^2} \end{bmatrix} $$

Because of Clairaut's Theorem, the Hessian is almost always Symmetric.

2. Interpretation: Curvature

If you think of the gradient as the "Velocity" of the surface change, the Hessian is the "Acceleration". A large Hessian value in a specific direction means the slope is changing very quickly there.

3. AI/ML Connection: Second-Order Optimization

The Spectral Radius and Stability

In Deep Learning, the Eigenvalues of the Hessian matrix determine the Maximum Learning Rate you can use without the model "Exploding".

If the largest eigenvalue is \(\lambda_{max}\), then your learning rate \(\eta\) should usually be less than \(2 / \lambda_{max}\). This is why researchers study the Hessian Spectrum. If the Hessian has very large eigenvalues, the loss landscape is like a "Sharp Ravine" where the model will bounce back and forth uncontrollably. AI architectures like Batch Norm work by suppressing these large Hessian values, making the landscape "flatter" and easier to optimize.

4. Python: Hessian via SymPy

import sympy as sp

x, y = sp.symbols('x y')
f = x**2 * y + sp.sin(x*y)

hessian = sp.hessian(f, (x, y))
print("Hessian Matrix:")
sp.pprint(hessian)

# Evaluate at (0, 0)
h_val = hessian.subs({x: 0, y: 0})
print(f"Hessian at origin: {h_val}")
